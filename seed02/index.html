A NOTE FROM THE PRESENT VERSION

before I become
someone else

I remember being terribly taken by the city.

The buildings that seemed to reach for the sky, the glitzy nights, the sudden blast of air when a local train pulled into the station. The streets that never quite went to sleep. The people who walked as though they had somewhere important to be, and probably did.

Mumbai had a particular sort of electricity about it. Not just the lights, or the traffic, or the rather impressive ability of a city to fit a thousand lives into a single glance. It was the ambition. You could almost feel it in the air: in the hurried conversations, the people building something after work, the people starting over, the people who had arrived with very little and were determined to make something of it.

Everyone seemed to be in motion.

And I, naturally, wanted a piece of everything.

I wanted to know what everybody was doing, where they were going, what they were building, what they knew that I didn't. I wanted to see the places, learn the things, meet the people, try my hand at whatever had caught my attention that week.

The city made possibility feel less like an abstract concept and more like something happening three streets away.

You never quite know what the day will bring. A conversation becomes a connection; a connection becomes an opportunity; an opportunity opens a door you hadn't known to look for. You leave the house as one version of yourself and return with a new idea, a new question, a slightly altered understanding of what might be possible.

I don't think I understood it then, but perhaps that was the beginning of something.

The realisation that a life can change in ways you could never have scheduled.

And that sometimes, the best things happen when you haven't quite worked out what you're doing yet.

A SMALL THING, WITH A GREAT DEAL INSIDE

why a pomegranate?

At home, my parents peel pomegranates for me.

It is an ordinary little act, which is perhaps why I have never quite known how to explain what it means. Someone sits down, patiently separates the ruby seeds from their pale, stubborn membranes, and leaves you with the part that is easiest to eat. No grand declaration. Just care, made practical; affection with its sleeves rolled up.

I think of that whenever I look at this archive. A pomegranate is a small universe held together by a skin: each seed its own bright, self-contained thing, and still part of something larger. A thought, a photograph, a passing obsession, a piece of work — separate little lives, gathered under one roof.

So the fruit stayed. Not because it was a perfect symbol, but because it was already ours.

A little universe, peeled with love.

FIELD NOTE 01A SMALL MATTER OF NEVER HAVING ENOUGH TABS OPEN

a small matter of never having enough tabs open

I have been calling myself Dora the Explorer for some time now.

Partly because I have an incurable urge to investigate anything that catches my attention. Partly because I have a longstanding affection for short hair, but that's a separate matter.

The more serious explanation is that I find it terribly difficult to leave a question alone.

Medicine leads to technology. Technology leads to the business of building things. A conversation about art takes me into history; history takes me into a book; the book leaves me with three more things to look up. Somewhere along the way, I have also decided that I would like to sing, make things, understand people, learn French, travel more, become a better writer, build something of my own, and perhaps develop a working knowledge of approximately everything.

A modest little list.

The trouble is that curiosity is excellent at generating new beginnings and rather less considerate about the time required to see them through.

There are only so many hours in a day. There is only so much of yourself you can give to a thing before another thing begins tugging at your sleeve. You start with the intention of giving something your absolute best, discover three other things worth doing, and suddenly find yourself conducting a small orchestra in which every instrument insists on being the soloist.

Something, inevitably, goes out of tune.

I have spent a fair amount of time trying to reconcile the number of things I want to do with the number of hours I have been given to do them. I haven't entirely succeeded.

Perhaps the trick isn't to become less curious. Perhaps it is to learn that choosing one thing for now doesn't mean abandoning every other thing forever.

An inconvenient lesson for someone who would quite like to do it all.

FIELD NOTE 02THE MANY GIRLS I'VE BEEN

the many girls I've been

Every few months, I look back at something I thought, wanted or believed, and find myself slightly astonished by the person who wrote it.

Sometimes she seems impossibly naive. Sometimes I want to reach through time, take her by the shoulders and explain a few things. Sometimes, rather embarrassingly, I realise she was onto something.

It is a strange thing to grow up in public, even when the audience is mostly just the people around you and the occasional future version of yourself.

I change my mind. I change my interests. I discover something new and become briefly, spectacularly consumed by it. I make plans with the conviction of someone who has finally figured everything out, only to find myself reconsidering them a few months later.

I adapt quickly. I can lose track of things just as quickly. There are versions of me that feel like they belong to entirely different chapters, and yet I can recognise the same little habits running through all of them.

The questions. The restlessness. The urge to understand.

I have always found stagnation a little unsettling. There is something about the idea of remaining exactly where I am, knowing only what I know today, that makes me want to get up and do something about it.

But movement has its own peculiarities. You can change so quickly that you scarcely notice what you have left behind. You can spend so much time becoming the next version of yourself that you forget to get acquainted with the one you already are.

And if every few months brings a new perspective, which version of me am I supposed to call the real one?

I am beginning to suspect that this is the wrong question.

Perhaps the earlier versions weren't mistakes. Perhaps they were simply working with what they knew. They were trying things, making sense of things, getting a few things spectacularly wrong and occasionally stumbling into something worth keeping.

I would like to be a little kinder to them.

After all, they got me here.

FIELD NOTE 03A CITY FULL OF PEOPLE WHO KNOW WHAT THEY'RE DOING

a city full of people who know what they're doing

(or at least look as though they do)

There is a particular feeling that comes with being surrounded by people who are very good at what they do.

everyone looks further along from a distance.

The founder who has been building something for years. The artist who sees things you would never have noticed. The doctor who seems to know the answer before you have finished asking the question. The person who walks into a room and makes networking look like the most natural thing in the world.

Mumbai is full of people like these. Or perhaps it simply gives you enough chances to encounter them.

I find it exhilarating. I also find it a little terrifying.

You begin to see just how much exists beyond the edges of your own experience. Entire industries you know very little about. Skills you have barely begun to acquire. People your age doing things you hadn't even thought to attempt.

And suddenly, the distance between where you are and where you could be feels enormous.

I have lost count of the times I have wanted to do something and then convinced myself I needed to be more prepared before I could begin. Read a little more. Learn a little more. Become a little more qualified, a little more articulate, a little more certain that I wouldn't make a fool of myself.

I have a remarkable talent for finding one more thing I ought to do before doing the thing itself.

Sometimes I call it preparation. Sometimes, if I am being honest, it is fear wearing a rather respectable outfit.

There is a word for the feeling that everyone else has somehow earned their place while you are still waiting for someone to notice that you haven't quite figured it out.

I've felt it. I suspect I will feel it again.

But I don't want to give it too much authority over my life.

Because the more people I meet, the more I realise that competence is not a permanent state of being. People are learning things while doing them. They are asking questions, making mistakes, figuring out the next step after they have already taken the first.

Some are more experienced than I am. Some have had different opportunities. Some are simply further along a particular path.

None of that means I have to wait until I am a finished product before I am allowed to participate.

I wish I had understood that sooner.

I am still learning it now.

FIELD NOTE 04THE CURIOUS BUSINESS OF BEING SEEN

the curious business of being seen

There is also the rather strange business of wanting to be seen and wanting to disappear in equal measure.

I want to make things, put my work out there, meet people, be part of interesting rooms and do things worth talking about. And then, almost instinctively, I want to retreat somewhere nobody is looking. To build quietly, keep my cards close, and perhaps emerge only when I have made something I am certain of.

visibility is not the same thing as exposure.

I have always been fascinated by people who seem to operate this way: quietly competent, almost deliberately unremarkable, with no particular need to announce what they are doing. The sort who are perfectly content to remain off the radar.

Meanwhile, I can be caught between wanting to put myself out there and wondering whether I have quite earned the right to.

Perhaps I want the visibility without the scrutiny, the opportunity without the exposure. Or perhaps I am still learning that being seen while I am figuring things out is not the same as being found out.

A rather inconvenient distinction, that.

FIELD NOTE 05ALL THE THINGS THAT HAPPENED BECAUSE ONE THING HAPPENED

all the things that happened because one thing happened

I am an introvert, which can be rather difficult to believe once I get going.

Getting going is the key phrase here.

I can be hesitant about approaching someone, initiating a conversation or walking into a room where I don't know quite what to expect. The first few minutes can feel like a disproportionately large undertaking.

And then, somehow, we start talking.

One question becomes another. We move from medicine to art, from someone's work to their childhood, from a passing observation to a conversation that has somehow lasted two hours. I have an unfortunate tendency to find a connection between almost anything and everything, and an equally unfortunate tendency to follow it wherever it leads.

I have been fortunate in the people this has brought into my life.

Some arrived through college. Some through projects, events, research or a shared interest. Some were people I might never have met had I not decided, on a particular day, to show up somewhere I was unsure about.

One experiment led to another. One introduction led to a conversation. One conversation led to a room I wouldn't otherwise have entered.

And then there were the people who helped me see something differently, introduced me to a new idea, encouraged me to try something, or simply made an ordinary day more interesting.

I am more grateful for these encounters than I probably say aloud.

I may not always be the person who calls, checks in, or keeps a conversation going. I have a terrible habit of disappearing into my own little world. But I remember things. A detail someone mentioned in passing, a story they told me once, something they were worried about, something they hoped to do.

I am, in that sense, a little nosy. In the most affectionate way possible.

People are endlessly interesting to me. Not just what they do, but how they think, what they notice, what they care about, what makes them laugh, what they are trying to become.

And the more people I meet, the less convinced I am that anybody can be understood from a distance.

There is always another layer. Another context. Something you could not have known from the first impression.

Perhaps this is why I am so grateful to the people who have crossed my path, even briefly. They have left me with more than memories. They have altered the range of things I can imagine.

I don't think we always realise how much of our lives is shaped by encounters we could never have planned.

Sometimes, all you do is turn up and talk to someone.

And then your life is a little different.

FIELD NOTE 06THE ART OF BEING IN TWO PLACES AT ONCE

the art of being in two places at once

One of the strangest things about living in Mumbai is how easily the city can make you feel both impossibly small and extraordinarily lucky.

There are days when I sit in a local train and simply watch.

The woman opposite me, holding a bag against her knees. The person who has fallen asleep despite the noise. The conversations happening in fragments. The faces at each station, appearing for a moment and disappearing into the crowd.

Everyone has somewhere to be. Everyone is carrying something, even if you cannot see what it is.

Sometimes I find myself wondering about their lives. What their mornings looked like. What they are going home to. What they have been working towards, what they have had to give up, what they are hoping will work out.

I feel grateful to be here, to be able to study, explore, meet people and make plans for a future that still feels so open.

And then there are moments when the contrast is difficult to ignore.

The city can take you from crowded compartments to beautiful buildings, from the ordinary urgency of getting through the day to places where time seems to have been given a rather generous budget. You can experience its grandest corners and, a little later, find yourself standing in the middle of its everyday struggles.

Neither experience cancels the other out.

I don't think the answer is to feel guilty for enjoying what you have, or to romanticise the difficulties other people face. The two are not interchangeable, and gratitude should not require us to pretend otherwise.

But I do think there is something valuable in being reminded of the scale of the world outside your own life.

The things that feel enormous to me can be very small in another person's day. The things I take for granted may be things somebody else has worked extraordinarily hard to obtain.

And the people I pass on the way to wherever I am going have lives as full, complicated and particular as my own.

I want to remember that.

To be ambitious without becoming oblivious. To enjoy the beautiful things without mistaking them for the whole world. To keep looking, even when looking makes me uncomfortable.

Perhaps that is one of the better things a city can teach you.

How to hold wonder and perspective in the same pair of hands.

FIELD NOTE 07A LITTLE FAITH IN THE PLOT

a little faith in the plot

For all my planning, overthinking and occasionally excessive attempts to anticipate the future, I have a quiet belief that the things meant for me will find a way of reaching me.

Not necessarily in the form I imagined. Not always on the timeline I would have preferred. And certainly not without a little confusion along the way.

I don't mean that everything happens for a reason, or that every disappointment is secretly a wonderful thing in disguise. Some things are simply difficult. Some doors close, and some experiences take time to understand, if they ever make complete sense at all.

But looking back, I can see how often one thing has led to another in ways I could not have predicted.

A decision I was uncertain about has introduced me to someone wonderful. Something I tried on a whim has taught me a skill I now use elsewhere. An experience that felt like a detour has left me with a perspective I would not otherwise have acquired.

I have been helped by people I hadn't expected to meet. I have found interests I didn't know I would love. I have changed my mind about things I once felt certain of.

Perhaps faith, for me, is not the belief that the future will unfold exactly as I want it to.

Perhaps it is the belief that I can keep moving towards it without needing to know every turn in advance.

That I can prepare, make decisions, take responsibility for my choices, and still leave a little room for the unexpected.

There is comfort in that.

I don't have to know what the next chapter is called before I begin reading it.

FIELD NOTE 08THE ROOMS I RETURN TO

the rooms I return to

For someone who is so easily enticed by the world outside, I have always had a rather strong attachment to being alone.

Solitude is not a consolation prize for me. It is something I actively seek out.

solitude is a room; it need not become a hiding place.

Give me a quiet afternoon, something to make or read, a little music, and the freedom to follow a thought without having to explain where it is going. I can spend hours in my own company and emerge feeling as though I have finally caught up with myself.

I have been this way for as long as I can remember. My parents put us into all sorts of activities growing up, and I am grateful for the exposure, but I have also always had an inward life that was entirely my own.

I like having the space to think. To notice what I feel about something after the excitement of experiencing it has passed. To revisit a conversation, reconsider an opinion, or make something simply because I want to see what happens.

Of course, there is a difference between solitude and retreating from everything that makes you uncomfortable.

I know I can retreat a little too readily. I can spend so much time thinking about an idea that the actual act of pursuing it begins to feel strangely distant. I can get lost in my own reflections, examining a decision from every possible angle until the decision itself has become exhausted.

Self-awareness is useful. Excessive self-surveillance is considerably less so.

Sometimes the kindest thing I can do for myself is stop analysing the experience and actually have it.

Go to the event. Start the conversation. Make the thing. Ask the question.

Not every moment needs to become a lesson before it is allowed to be a moment.

I am trying to remember that, too.

FIELD NOTE 09A SMALL WORLD, BY CHOICE OR OTHERWISE

a small world, by choice or otherwise

Friendships are a slightly complicated subject for me.

I have never been particularly good at keeping them alive across distance, time or the general business of life. A handful from school, a couple that remain now, and an embarrassing number of people I remember fondly but have not spoken to in ages.

remembering someone and reaching out are, regrettably, different skills.

I could blame it on being busy, or on my rather impressive ability to disappear into my own world. The truth is, reaching out does not always come naturally to me. I can remember the smallest details about someone and still somehow fail to send a simple hey, how have you been?

I know this is a debatable philosophy, and perhaps an unfair one to friendships themselves. I sometimes struggle to understand the need to keep a conversation going simply because it has been a while (the sunk-cost fallacy peeking in), yet I know relationships require a little tending, even when nothing particularly urgent needs to be said.

Perhaps I have mistaken being comfortable alone for not needing to make the effort.

I am still figuring it out.

FIELD NOTE 10A LIFE THAT KEEPS CHANGING ITS MIND

a life that keeps changing its mind

Sometimes I think I have experienced more of life in these three years than I have spent learning medicine.

Which is a slightly alarming thing to say as a medical student, but I think I know what I mean.

There has been the formal education, of course. The lectures, the wards, the patients, the examinations, the slow accumulation of knowledge that eventually begins to resemble understanding.

But there has also been everything happening around it.

The people. The conversations. The projects. The things I have attempted without knowing whether I would be any good at them. The places I have discovered. The things I have read, the things I have made, the things I have wanted to do and the things I have finally dared to try.

Medicine has been one part of my education. The rest has been a rather unruly curriculum.

I have learnt things about people that no textbook could have taught me in quite the same way. I have seen how differently people approach the same problem, how much of a person's life exists outside the role they occupy in a room, how often the most interesting learning happens when you are willing to ask a question that might sound obvious.

I have learnt that ambition can be infectious, that confidence can be practised, and that the people who seem most certain of their direction are not necessarily the people who have never been lost.

I have also learnt that wanting to do something and being ready to do it are not always the same thing. That a full calendar can create the illusion of a full life. That there is a difference between being interested in many things and giving those things enough attention to become good at them.

I am still working that one out.

But I would not trade the wandering for a neater story.

Not because every detour has been useful, or every experiment has worked, but because I have begun to understand that the person I am becoming is shaped by more than the subject I study or the profession I eventually enter.

I want to be good at medicine. I want to make things, ask questions, build something worthwhile and remain curious about the world outside whatever I happen to be doing at the time.

I want to keep the range without losing the depth.

The trick, I suspect, will be learning when to explore and when to stay long enough to understand what I have found.

FIELD NOTE 11TO THE GIRL WHO WILL READ THIS LATER

to the girl who will read this later

I know you.

Or at least, I know that by the time you read this, you will probably have found several things to disagree with.

Perhaps you will think the writing was a little earnest. Perhaps you will wonder why I felt the need to explain things that now seem perfectly obvious. Perhaps you will have abandoned half the plans I am currently so excited about and taken up something I have not even considered yet.

I can practically hear you clearing your throat.

You may also have forgotten some of the things I am writing about here. The people, the conversations, the little moments that seem so significant now. You have a tendency to move on quickly, to become absorbed in whatever has caught your attention next, and to assume that the things you care about today will remain just as vivid tomorrow.

They don't always.

Which is partly why I am writing this down.

Not to preserve a version of myself that I expect to remain unchanged, but to remember what it felt like to be her.

To remember the city that seemed to contain a thousand possible lives. The people who made those possibilities feel real. The things I was afraid to attempt, the things I tried anyway, and the strange mixture of gratitude and inadequacy that accompanied so much of it.

I hope you have become a little braver about beginning before you feel entirely ready.

I hope you have learnt to give things your full attention without feeling that every other interest has been lost to you forever.

I hope you still make time to be alone, still notice the little things, still find yourself absorbed in an unexpected subject at an inconvenient hour.

And I hope you have not mistaken becoming more capable for becoming less curious.

Because that is the thing I would most like to keep.

The willingness to be surprised. The readiness to change my mind. The belief that there is always something more to see, learn, make or understand.

Perhaps you will have changed beyond recognition in some ways. Perhaps you will look back at this and wince at the earnestness of it all.

That's alright.

I am sure I will do the same when I read it again in six months.

But I hope that somewhere beneath the new interests, the abandoned plans, the things you have learnt and the person you have become, you will recognise the same girl.

The one who wanted to know a little about everything.

The one who was sometimes afraid to begin, but wanted to begin anyway.

The one who could sit alone for hours and still be endlessly fascinated by the world outside.

The one who kept looking.

I don't know exactly where all this is going. For once, I think I am willing to let that be enough.

After all, I have never been particularly good at sitting still.

And the world, inconveniently, is rather large.

There is so much left to see.
