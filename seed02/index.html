A NOTE FROM THE PRESENT VERSION

before I figure it all out

I have a slightly unreasonable relationship with possibility.

Give me an interesting idea and I will immediately begin imagining all the things I could do with it. Give me an interesting person, profession, book, city or field of study and I will want to know enough about it to have an opinion. I am particularly susceptible to the discovery that there is an entire world I have not yet explored, which, as it turns out, is most of the world.

Mumbai hasn't exactly helped.

There is something about living in a city where so much is happening, where ambition is practically a public utility, that makes possibility feel unusually close. You can be going about an entirely ordinary day and suddenly find yourself thinking about a life you hadn't considered five minutes earlier.

Everyone seemed to be in motion.

And I, naturally, wanted a piece of everything.

I wanted to know what everybody was doing, where they were going, what they were building, what they knew that I didn't. I wanted to see the places, learn the things, try my hand at whatever had caught my attention that week. I wanted to understand medicine, technology, art, business, the brain, the world and approximately everything in between.

The city made possibility feel less like an abstract concept and more like something happening three streets away.

The trouble is that possibility is an excellent thing to have in theory. In practice, every possible life comes with its own set of decisions, trade-offs, unglamorous hours and things you have to get rather good at before anyone finds them interesting.

I am much better at imagining the possibilities than I would sometimes like to admit.

And yet, I keep coming back to the same thought: a life can change in ways you could never have scheduled. Sometimes, the best things happen when you haven't quite worked out what you're doing yet.

I would like to get better at letting that happen without needing to have the entire thing figured out first.

A SMALL THING, WITH A GREAT DEAL INSIDE

why a pomegranate?

At home, my parents peel pomegranates for me.

The fruit found its way into this archive partly because of that, and partly because I like what it represents. A small universe, held together by a skin. Hundreds of individual seeds, each with its own shape and colour, gathered into one thing.

I think of each seed as an idea, an interest, a version of myself, or something I haven't quite figured out what to do with yet. They don't all have to become something immediately. They can simply exist here, together.

Not everything needs to lead somewhere to be worth keeping.

A little universe, peeled with love.

FIELD NOTE 01A SMALL MATTER OF NEVER HAVING ENOUGH TABS OPEN

a small matter of never having enough tabs open

I have been calling myself Dora the Explorer for some time now.

Partly because I have an incurable urge to investigate anything that catches my attention. Partly because I have a longstanding affection for short hair, but that's a separate matter.

The more serious explanation is that I find it terribly difficult to leave a question alone.

Medicine leads to technology. Technology leads to the business of building things. Art takes me into history, history into a book, and the book leaves me with several more things to look up. I want to sing, make things, understand people, learn French, travel, write better, build something of my own and perhaps acquire a working knowledge of approximately everything.

A modest little list.

The trouble is that curiosity is excellent at generating new beginnings and rather less considerate about the time required to see them through.

There are only so many hours in a day. There is only so much of yourself you can give to a thing before another thing begins tugging at your sleeve. You start with the intention of giving something your absolute best, discover three other things worth doing, and suddenly find yourself conducting a small orchestra in which every instrument insists on being the soloist.

Something, inevitably, goes out of tune.

For a long time, I thought the answer was to become better at managing everything. Better schedules, better systems, better discipline. If I could just organise my life correctly, perhaps I could fit all the things I wanted into it.

I have made some rather lovely plans. My actual life has not always been as cooperative.

I am beginning to understand that range is not the same as doing everything. It is not collecting interests like little souvenirs and calling the collection a life. There is a difference between being curious about something, trying it, and staying with it long enough to understand it properly.

An inconvenient lesson for someone who would quite like to do it all.

On range

David Epstein's Range gave me a useful way to think about something I had been feeling for a while: the idea that a broad set of interests and experiences can be valuable in its own right, rather than evidence that you have failed to commit to one thing.

The book explores how generalists can thrive, particularly in complex environments where the problem isn't clearly defined and the answer isn't already sitting in a textbook. Trying different things can help you recognise patterns, connect ideas that usually live in separate compartments and approach a problem from an angle someone else might miss.

I find that idea deeply reassuring.

Not because I want a convenient intellectual justification for every hobby I've ever picked up, or because breadth automatically makes someone exceptional. It doesn't. Having ten interests and being good at none of them is not a particularly impressive career strategy.

But there is something to be said for the person who understands enough about different fields to notice where they might intersect. Medicine and design. Biology and engineering. Science and business. A clinical problem and a technology that might actually help solve it.

I don't want to have to amputate parts of my curiosity just to make myself easier to explain.

At the same time, I know that range needs somewhere to go. Ideas need attention. Skills need practice. Projects need to survive the rather unromantic middle stage after the initial excitement wears off.

Perhaps the goal is not to choose between being a generalist and becoming good at something. Perhaps it is to build enough depth in the right places that all the other things I know can become useful.

I am still working out what that looks like for me.

I would like to stop treating every interest I cannot pursue immediately as a life I have somehow missed out on.

FIELD NOTE 02THE MANY GIRLS I'VE BEEN

the many girls I've been

Every few months, I look back at something I wanted, wrote or believed and feel a little astonished by the person who left it there.

Sometimes she seems naive. Sometimes I want to take her aside and explain a few things. And sometimes, rather annoyingly, she was right.

It is a strange thing to grow up in public, even when the audience is mostly just the people around you and the occasional future version of yourself.

I change my mind. I change my interests. I discover something new and become briefly, spectacularly consumed by it. I make plans with the conviction of someone who has finally figured everything out, only to find myself reconsidering them a few months later.

There are versions of me that feel as though they belong to entirely different chapters. The girl who was certain about one thing, the girl who wanted something completely different, the girl who had an elaborate plan for her future and the girl who abandoned it after discovering a more interesting possibility.

I can be quite a difficult person to keep up with, even for myself.

But there are things that have remained remarkably consistent. The questions. The restlessness. The urge to understand. The impatience I feel when I think I am standing still.

I have spent a fair amount of time trying to become a better version of myself. Sometimes that has meant learning something, making a difficult decision or finally doing something I had been putting off. Other times, it has meant looking at myself as though I were an unfinished project that required constant revision.

There is always something to improve. Something to fix. Something I could be doing better.

It is possible to become so preoccupied with your potential that you stop paying attention to the person who has to live through the process of reaching it.

I don't want to spend my entire life treating the present version of myself as an inconvenience.

The earlier versions weren't drafts to be embarrassed by. They were working with what they knew. They tried things, got things wrong, changed their minds and occasionally left me something worth keeping.

I would like to be a little kinder to them.

After all, they got me here.

FIELD NOTE 03A CITY FULL OF PEOPLE WHO KNOW WHAT THEY'RE DOING

a city full of people who know what they're doing

(or at least look as though they do)

There is a particular feeling that comes with being surrounded by people who are very good at what they do.

everyone looks further along from a distance.

The founder who has spent years building something. The artist who sees things you would never have noticed. The doctor who seems to understand the shape of a problem before you have finished describing it. The person who walks into a room and makes everything look easy.

I find it exhilarating. I also find it a little terrifying.

You begin to notice how much exists beyond the edges of your own experience. Entire industries you know very little about. Skills you have barely begun to acquire. People your age doing things you hadn't even thought to attempt.

And suddenly, the distance between where you are and where you could be feels enormous.

I have a remarkable talent for finding one more thing I ought to do before doing the thing itself.

Read more. Prepare more. Learn another skill. Make the plan a little better. Become a little more qualified, articulate, certain. Wait until I have a clearer idea of what I'm doing. Wait until I know enough to be taken seriously.

Sometimes this is sensible preparation. Sometimes, if I am being honest, it is fear wearing a rather respectable outfit.

I know what it feels like to want something badly and still find reasons not to begin. To imagine doing something, mentally rehearse every possible version of it, anticipate what might go wrong and exhaust myself with a task I haven't actually attempted.

The frustrating part is that, from the outside, it can look like ambition. I am thinking about my future. I am researching. I am planning. I am trying to get better.

But there is a point at which preparation stops being preparation and becomes a very convincing way of avoiding the possibility of failure.

If I don't submit the application, the rejection hasn't happened. If I don't put the work out there, nobody can tell me it isn't good enough. If I keep researching an idea instead of testing it, I can continue to believe it might be brilliant.

Potential is a lovely place to live. Nothing has gone wrong there yet.

Reality, unfortunately, requires you to do things.

I have felt that particular flavour of imposter syndrome where you suspect everyone else has earned their place while you are still waiting to be found out. Where someone else's competence becomes evidence of your inadequacy. Where you can recognise all the things you have yet to learn much more readily than you can recognise what you already bring to the table.

I've felt it. I suspect I will feel it again.

But I don't want to give it too much authority over my life.

The people I admire are not necessarily free of doubt. They have simply had more time to practise, more chances to fail, or the willingness to begin without knowing exactly how things will turn out.

I cannot think my way into becoming someone who has done the work. Eventually, I have to do the work.

I am trying to learn how to begin before I feel entirely ready. To let the first attempt be imperfect. To be a beginner without turning that fact into a referendum on my potential.

It is uncomfortable. It is also probably the only way out.

FIELD NOTE 04THE CURIOUS BUSINESS OF BEING SEEN

the curious business of being seen

There is also the rather strange business of wanting to be seen and wanting to disappear in equal measure.

I want to make things, share my work, build something of my own and do things worth talking about. Then another part of me wants to retreat somewhere nobody is looking, work quietly, keep my cards close and emerge only when I am certain I have something worth showing.

I admire people who are quietly competent, almost deliberately unremarkable, with no particular need to announce every step. The sort who are perfectly content to remain off the radar.

At the same time, I know that keeping everything private until it is perfect can become a remarkably effective way of keeping it invisible.

I think about the things I want to do and the person I imagine myself becoming. I can see the possibilities clearly enough. What is less clear is why I sometimes find it so difficult to let the actual world see me trying.

Part of it is imposter syndrome. Part of it is perfectionism. Part of it is the very human desire to be admired without first having to risk being unimpressive.

I want the opportunity without the scrutiny. The achievement without the awkward beginning. The confidence that comes from having done something without the vulnerability of doing it for the first time.

Which is, of course, a spectacularly convenient arrangement that the universe has not agreed to.

I can be hard on myself in ways that would sound unreasonable if I said them aloud. I know how easily I compare the messy, unfinished reality of my own life with the polished version of someone else's. I know how quickly admiration can turn into a private inventory of everything I think I lack.

And I know that, sometimes, I hold myself back before anyone else has had the chance to.

I don't always need someone to tell me I cannot do something. I am perfectly capable of constructing the argument myself.

Maybe I want to protect the parts of my life that are mine. Maybe I am afraid of being judged while I am still figuring things out. Maybe I have mistaken being capable of doing something for needing to be certain I can do it well before I begin.

Probably a little of everything.

I don't have a neat resolution for this. I am still learning to distinguish between wanting privacy and hiding, between being thoughtful and being afraid, between genuinely preparing and finding increasingly sophisticated reasons to wait.

visibility is not the same thing as exposure.

And being seen while I am figuring things out is not the same as being found out.

I would like to remember that the next time I am tempted to wait until I have become an entirely different person before allowing this one to participate.

FIELD NOTE 05ALL THE THINGS THAT HAPPENED BECAUSE ONE THING HAPPENED

all the things that happened because one thing happened

I am an introvert, which can be rather difficult to believe once I get going.

Getting going is the key phrase here.

I can be hesitant about approaching someone, starting a conversation or walking into a room where I don't know quite what to expect. The first few minutes can feel like a disproportionately large undertaking.

And then, somehow, we start talking.

One question becomes another. We move from medicine to art, from someone's work to an entirely unrelated subject, and suddenly two hours have gone by.

I have been fortunate in the opportunities that have come from being willing to show up, even when I wasn't entirely sure I belonged there. Some of the things that have shaped my life began with a decision that felt fairly ordinary at the time.

One experiment led to another. One introduction led to an opportunity I wouldn't otherwise have encountered. Something I tried on a whim became a skill I now use elsewhere. An experience that felt like a detour left me with a perspective I would not otherwise have acquired.

I am grateful for that.

I also know that I can be so focused on what comes next that I fail to notice what is happening while it is happening. I can be thinking about the next opportunity while standing in the middle of one. I can be so occupied with what an experience might lead to that I forget to enjoy the experience itself.

There is something to be said for showing up without needing to extract a lesson, a contact, a credential or a career opportunity from every single thing.

Not every conversation needs to change your life. Not every interest needs to become a project. Not every afternoon has to be productive to justify its existence.

Sometimes an experience can simply be interesting.

I would like to get better at that, too.

FIELD NOTE 06THE ART OF BEING IN TWO PLACES AT ONCE

the art of being in two places at once

One of the strange things about living in Mumbai is how quickly the city can make you feel both very small and very lucky.

Sometimes I sit on a local train and watch the details go by: a woman holding her bag against her knees, someone asleep despite the noise, conversations happening in fragments, faces appearing at one station and disappearing at the next.

Everyone has somewhere to be. Everyone is carrying something, even if you cannot see what it is.

I find myself wondering about the lives around me: what people are going home to, what they are working towards, what they have had to put aside, what they hope will work out.

I am grateful for the life I get to lead. For the education I am receiving, the things I have been able to explore, the people who have encouraged me, and the fact that I can imagine so many possible futures.

I also know that the city can take you from a crowded compartment to a beautiful building in the space of an afternoon, and make the differences between those worlds difficult to ignore.

I don't think the answer is to feel guilty for enjoying what you have, or to turn other people's difficulties into a lesson about your own gratitude. Their lives are not there to make mine feel meaningful.

But it is worth remembering that the things I take for granted may have required someone else an extraordinary amount of effort. That access, time, money, safety and opportunity are not distributed evenly. That the future I spend so much time imagining is not equally available to everyone.

I want to be ambitious without becoming oblivious. To enjoy the beautiful things without mistaking them for the whole world. To keep looking, even when looking complicates the story I would rather tell myself.

FIELD NOTE 07THE HEDONIC TREADMILL

the hedonic treadmill

There is a concept in psychology called the hedonic treadmill: the tendency for people to adapt to changes in their circumstances, so that the happiness or satisfaction a new achievement brings often fades as it becomes part of ordinary life.

You want something. You work towards it. You get it. For a little while, it feels wonderful.

Then it becomes normal.

And, before long, there is something else you want.

I find this idea both fascinating and mildly offensive.

It seems to describe a rather familiar pattern. The next milestone, the next achievement, the next version of yourself that will finally make you feel as though you have arrived. You imagine that reaching a particular point will settle something inside you, that you will feel more capable, more secure, more certain of yourself.

Sometimes it does help. Achievements matter. Progress matters. There are real things worth working towards, and I don't want to pretend otherwise.

But there is a difference between wanting to grow and believing that you will finally be allowed to feel good about yourself once you have grown enough.

I can see how easily the two get tangled.

There is always another examination, another project, another opportunity, another skill to acquire. There is always someone who has done more, someone who seems further ahead, some new version of success that makes the last one feel a little less impressive.

Even rest can become an achievement. Even self-improvement can turn into another way of keeping score.

You start trying to optimise your life and somehow end up treating your life as a problem to be solved.

I don't think ambition is the enemy here. I like wanting things. I like being excited about what I might be able to do. I want to build things, learn things, travel, make work I am proud of and become more capable than I am today.

What worries me is the possibility of becoming so preoccupied with the next thing that I never properly inhabit the thing I have already worked for.

I don't want every good moment to become a stepping stone towards a better one. I don't want to look back at a period of my life and realise I was too busy trying to improve it to actually enjoy it.

There is a version of ambition that makes your world larger. There is another that makes the goalposts move every time you get close.

I am trying to work out how to tell the difference.

Perhaps contentment isn't the opposite of ambition. Perhaps it is what allows ambition to remain something you choose, rather than something you are forever trying to satisfy.

I would like to be able to want more without making the present feel inadequate.

FIELD NOTE 08THE ROOMS I RETURN TO

the rooms I return to

For someone so easily enticed by the world outside, I have always been quite attached to being alone.

Solitude is not a consolation prize for me. It is something I actively seek out.

solitude is a room; it need not become a hiding place.

Give me a quiet afternoon, something to read or make, a little music, and the freedom to follow a thought without having to explain where it is going. I can spend hours in my own company and emerge feeling as though I have caught up with myself.

I like the space to notice what I think about something after the excitement has passed. To revisit an opinion, reconsider a decision, or make something simply because I want to see what happens. My parents put us into all sorts of activities growing up, and I am grateful for that, but I have always had an inward life that was entirely my own.

There is a comfort in not having to perform a version of yourself for anyone else. In being able to change your mind privately, follow a thought to its rather strange conclusion, or spend an afternoon doing something that has no obvious purpose.

Of course, there is a difference between solitude and retreating from everything that makes you uncomfortable.

I know I can retreat a little too readily. I can think about an idea until the actual act of pursuing it begins to feel strangely distant. I can examine a decision from so many angles that the decision itself becomes exhausted.

Self-awareness is useful. Excessive self-surveillance is considerably less so.

Sometimes the kindest thing I can do is stop analysing an experience and actually have it: go to the event, start the conversation, make the thing, ask the question.

Not every moment needs to become a lesson before it is allowed to be a moment.

FIELD NOTE 09A SMALL WORLD, BY CHOICE OR OTHERWISE

a small world, by choice or otherwise

Friendships are a slightly complicated subject for me.

I have never been particularly good at keeping them alive across distance, time or the general business of life. There are people from school I still think of fondly, people I have grown closer to, and an embarrassing number of people I mean to message and somehow don't.

I can remember the smallest detail someone told me and still fail to send a simple hey, how have you been?

I disappear into my own world, get absorbed in whatever I am doing, and assume that affection will somehow communicate itself without any maintenance.

It doesn't always work that way.

I sometimes struggle with the idea of keeping a conversation going simply because time has passed. But I know relationships are not projects that should only receive attention when something urgent happens. A message can be a small way of saying: I thought of you. You still exist in my world.

I am comfortable alone. I like my own company. But I don't want to confuse that with being entirely self-sufficient, or pretend that the people in my life don't matter simply because I don't always know how to show it.

Perhaps I have mistaken remembering someone for keeping them close.

I am still figuring out how to care about people in ways they can actually feel, rather than only in the private archive of my own head.

FIELD NOTE 10A LIFE THAT KEEPS CHANGING ITS MIND

a life that keeps changing its mind

Sometimes I think I have learnt as much about life outside medicine as I have from studying it.

Which is a slightly alarming thing to say as a medical student, but I think I know what I mean.

There are the lectures, wards, patients and examinations, the slow accumulation of knowledge that eventually begins to resemble understanding.

And then there is everything happening around it: the things I have tried, the interests I have followed, the work I have attempted without knowing whether I would be any good at it, the ideas that have made me look at a familiar problem differently.

Medicine has been one part of my education. The rest has been a rather unruly curriculum.

I have learnt that wanting to do something and giving it enough attention to become good at it are two different things. That a full calendar can create the illusion of a full life. That being interested in many things is not the same as knowing any of them deeply.

I have also learnt that the way forward is rarely as clear from the inside as it looks in retrospect.

I want to be good at medicine. I want to make things, ask questions, build something worthwhile and remain curious about the world outside whatever I happen to be doing.

I don't want to trade the range for a neat, easily explained version of myself. I also don't want to use curiosity as an excuse to keep starting over.

I want to learn when to explore and when to stay long enough to understand what I have found. When to let an interest remain an interest, and when to give it the time and attention required to become something more.

I am not especially good at this yet.

But perhaps that is a more useful place to start than pretending I have already figured it out.

FIELD NOTE 11TWENTY-ONE, APPARENTLY

twenty-one, apparently

I turn twenty-one in December.

Writing that feels slightly strange.

Last year, turning twenty felt eerie in a way I still cannot entirely explain. It wasn't that anything dramatic changed overnight. I woke up the same person, with the same unfinished plans and the same fairly extensive list of things I thought I ought to be doing.

But twenty sounded different from nineteen.

Perhaps it was the sense that I was moving into an age at which people expected me to be a little more mindful, a little more responsible, a little more certain of myself. That I should be making better decisions, taking my future more seriously, knowing what I wanted and behaving accordingly.

All the usual adult things, apparently.

I don't know why it unsettled me so much. Maybe it was the realisation that growing up isn't a single event you can prepare for. There isn't a morning when you wake up and discover that you have acquired the judgement, emotional stability and general competence that everyone seems to associate with being an adult.

You just keep living. You make decisions with incomplete information. You get things wrong, learn a little, change your mind, and eventually find yourself expected to make another decision.

Now twenty-one is approaching, and I am not sure I feel any more prepared.

I still have days when I feel capable of doing something ambitious and days when I can turn a relatively ordinary task into an elaborate exercise in self-doubt. I have plans I am excited about, things I have been putting off, things I know I need to work on, and things I have not yet figured out how to want properly.

I am aware of time in a way I wasn't when I was younger. Not in the sense that twenty-one is old, because it plainly isn't, but in the sense that the years have started to feel more tangible. They are not an endless supply of future opportunities waiting politely for me to get around to them.

I can spend an entire afternoon imagining what I might do with my life. I can research an idea, make a plan, reconsider the plan, improve the plan and then go to bed feeling as though I have made progress.

Sometimes I have. Sometimes I have merely spent the day thinking about progress.

I don't want to turn this birthday into another deadline by which I must have become a more impressive person. I have enough of those in my head already.

But I do want to take seriously the fact that I am the one who has to live this life. Nobody else can make the decisions for me, do the difficult work, or give me back the time I spent waiting to feel ready.

Perhaps growing up has less to do with becoming certain and more to do with learning to act despite uncertainty.

I would like twenty-one to be the year I practise that.

Not by becoming a perfectly disciplined, relentlessly productive, impossibly self-aware person. That sounds exhausting, and I suspect I would find a way to turn it into another performance review.

Just by beginning a little more often.

By doing some of the things I keep thinking about. By letting an imperfect attempt teach me what another month of preparation cannot. By taking responsibility for my choices without treating every mistake as proof that I am not quite who I thought I was.

I don't know if that is what turning twenty-one is supposed to feel like.

But it is what I would like it to mean to me.

FIELD NOTE 12TO THE GIRL WHO WILL READ THIS LATER

to the girl who will read this later

I know you.

Or at least, I know that by the time you read this, you will probably have found several things to disagree with.

Perhaps the writing will seem a little earnest. Perhaps you will have abandoned half the plans I am currently excited about and taken up something I have not even considered yet. I can practically hear you clearing your throat.

You may have forgotten some of the things that feel significant now: the people, the conversations, the small moments that changed how a day went. You tend to move on quickly, absorbed by whatever has caught your attention next, and assume that today's interests will remain just as vivid tomorrow.

They don't always. Which is partly why I am writing this down. Not to preserve a version of myself I expect to remain unchanged, but to remember what it felt like to be her.

I hope you have become a little braver about beginning before you feel entirely ready. I hope you have learnt to give things your attention without feeling that every other interest has been lost to you forever.

I hope you have stopped confusing preparation with progress quite so often. I hope you can recognise the difference between a genuinely useful pause and the elaborate mental gymnastics you perform when you are afraid to try.

I hope you have learnt to be ambitious without making every day a referendum on whether you are doing enough.

I hope you still make time to be alone, still notice the little things, still find yourself absorbed in an unexpected subject at an inconvenient hour.

Most of all, I hope you have not mistaken becoming more capable for becoming less curious.

You will have changed. Of course you will. I hope that somewhere beneath the new interests, the abandoned plans and the things you have learnt, you recognise the same girl: the one who wanted to know a little about everything, who was sometimes afraid to begin but wanted to begin anyway, who could sit alone for hours and still be fascinated by the world outside.

I hope you are kinder to her than she has sometimes been to herself.

And if you have not done everything you thought you would by now, I hope you can resist the urge to turn that into evidence against yourself.

There will always be more to do. There will always be someone further along, something else to learn, another version of success that seems to demand your attention.

The world is not going to run out of possibilities just because you have chosen one for now.

I don't know exactly where all this is going. For once, I am willing to let that be enough.

After all, I have never been particularly good at sitting still.

And the world, inconveniently, is rather large.

There is so much left to see.
