A NOTE IN THE MARGIN
FROM GAURI'S DESK

BEFORE WE BEGIN

History was
the curriculum.
The images stayed.

I did rather well in social studies, which is funny because, at the time, I was mostly trying to get through the syllabus. What stayed wasn't a neat sequence of dates. It was the scale of it all: Rome expanding and cracking under its own weight; Greek gods behaving like the most dysfunctional family imaginable; wars, revolutions, empires, cities built to outlast the people who commissioned them.

And then there were the things you could see. A marble hand. A carved doorway. Hieroglyphs that had waited centuries to be read. A palace wall crowded with tiny figures. The strange feeling that a building in Fort or Ballard Estate was still holding on to an earlier Bombay, even as traffic and people moved around it.

Looking back, I think the fascination was never just with what happened. It was with the evidence that something happened: the material left behind, the image that survived, the little detail that outlived its explanation.

What all is hidden from us? A few pages can compress decades into a paragraph. The people who mixed the paint, carried the stone, copied the manuscript, cleaned the studio, paid for the commission, or made a brilliant thing that history forgot often disappear from the frame. One day, the AI revolution will be a paragraph in a school textbook, too. Imagine the lives that paragraph will flatten.

This is not a complete history of art. It is a collection of ways into looking: the famous names and the overlooked hands; the colour that travelled; the garment that shaped a body; the ordinary scene someone decided was worth painting. I am interested in the moment a familiar thing becomes strange again, and in the people and materials hiding just outside the frame.

01 / THE MOVING PICTURE

Art history,
in periods.

A map to wander through—not a list to memorise. The dates are approximate, movements overlap, and different parts of the world followed their own timelines. Here are the periods, a few artists, and works worth looking up.

FOLLOW THE THREAD

Start anywhere. Notice how images change with belief, materials, technology, politics and ordinary life. This is a compact, selective timeline—not a claim that every culture fits into one sequence.

EXPLORE BY ERA
c. 40,000 BCE onwardPREHISTORIC ART
Bhimbetka · marks across generations

01 · THE FIRST IMAGES

Before anyone called it art

At Bhimbetka, painted animals, human figures and marks survive on rock shelters. We can study their shapes and pigments, but we cannot always know precisely what each image meant to the person who made it. The first chapter of art history is full of images—and questions.

Look for: Bhimbetka rock shelters, the cave paintings at Lascaux, and the animal forms at Chauvet.

Names to know: The makers are mostly unknown. That is part of the story: the work survived even when the names did not.

c. 3000 BCE – 500 CEANCIENT CIVILISATIONS
Augustus of Prima Porta · Rome

02 · EGYPT, GREECE, ROME & SOUTH ASIA

Gods, rulers and ideal bodies

Ancient art was made for tombs, temples, homes, public spaces and political life. Egyptian artists made images for religious and funerary settings; Greek sculptors explored idealised bodies; Roman artists used portraits and monuments to make authority visible. Across South Asia, the Indus Valley civilisation, Mauryan pillars, Buddhist stupas and early sculpture developed distinct visual worlds.

Artists / makers: Phidias and Polykleitos are associated with Classical Greek sculpture; many Egyptian and South Asian works are anonymous or workshop-made.

Works to look up: Bust of Nefertiti, the Doryphoros, Augustus of Prima Porta, the Great Stupa at Sanchi, and Indus Valley seals.

c. 300–1400BYZANTINE, MEDIEVAL & SACRED ART
Ajanta · Bodhisattva Padmapani

03 · ART IN LIVED SPACES

Light, devotion and stories on walls

In many places, art was woven into architecture, worship, manuscripts and public ritual rather than separated into galleries. Byzantine mosaics shimmered in churches; Gothic cathedrals filtered coloured light through glass; Ajanta's murals filled rock-cut spaces with expressive figures. Chola bronzes could be carried in procession, meeting viewers in movement.

Artists / makers: Giotto helped shift Italian painting toward more bodily, emotionally legible figures. Most sacred works were made by workshops whose individual members are not all known.

Works to look up: Ajanta's Padmapani, the mosaics of Ravenna, the Book of Kells, and Giotto's Arena Chapel frescoes.

c. 1300–1600RENAISSANCE
Michelangelo · Pietà

04 · PERSPECTIVE, ANATOMY & HUMANISM

Artists become famous by name

In Renaissance Italy, artists, scholars and patrons renewed their interest in classical texts, observation and the study of the human body. Linear perspective helped create convincing depth; oil painting enabled new effects of light and detail. Workshops remained collaborative, but some artists became celebrated public figures.

Artists: Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Raphael, Sandro Botticelli, Titian, and Northern Renaissance painter Jan van Eyck.

Works to look up: The Last Supper, the Sistine Chapel ceiling, the Birth of Venus, the Mona Lisa, and the Arnolfini Portrait.

c. 1520–1600MANNERISM
El Greco · elongated bodies and spiritual intensity

05 · AFTER THE HIGH RENAISSANCE

When perfection starts to bend

Mannerist artists stretched bodies, tilted spaces and used surprising colours and complex poses. Instead of seeking only balanced harmony, they made images feel elegant, tense, artificial or uncanny. It is a reminder that artists often begin by testing the rules their predecessors made look effortless.

Artists: El Greco, Pontormo, Parmigianino, and Bronzino.

Works to look up: El Greco's The Burial of the Count of Orgaz and Parmigianino's Madonna with the Long Neck.

c. 1600–1750BAROQUE
Caravaggio · The Calling of Saint Matthew

06 · DRAMA & MOVEMENT

Make the viewer feel it

Baroque art is theatrical: hard light, deep shadows, diagonals, motion and emotional intensity pull the viewer into the scene. Caravaggio made sacred stories feel startlingly close; Bernini carved marble into bodies that seem to twist and breathe; Rembrandt used light to reveal and conceal; Artemisia Gentileschi painted powerful, psychologically charged figures.

Artists: Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, Gian Lorenzo Bernini, Rembrandt, and Peter Paul Rubens.

Works to look up: The Calling of Saint Matthew, Judith Slaying Holofernes, Ecstasy of Saint Teresa, and The Night Watch.

c. 1715–1780ROCOCO
Fragonard · The Swing

07 · ORNAMENT & LEISURE

Silk, flirtation and a world at play

Rococo favoured playful scenes, decorative interiors, pastel colours, curling forms and the pleasures of aristocratic life. Fragonard's The Swing is charming and mischievous—but the carefree world it depicts belonged to a privileged class, just before that social order came under intense pressure.

Artists: Jean-Honoré Fragonard, Antoine Watteau, and François Boucher.

Works to look up: The Swing, Pilgrimage to Cythera, and Diana Leaving the Bath.

c. 1750–1850NEOCLASSICISM & ROMANTICISM
Delacroix · Liberty Leading the People

08 · ORDER, REVOLUTION & THE SUBLIME

Two ways to answer a changing world

Neoclassicism looked to ancient Greece and Rome for clear forms, restraint and civic virtue. Romanticism turned toward emotion, imagination, political upheaval and the overwhelming force of nature. These were not clean opposites, but different responses to a rapidly changing world.

Artists: Jacques-Louis David and Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres (Neoclassicism); Francisco Goya, Eugène Delacroix, Caspar David Friedrich, J. M. W. Turner, and Théodore Géricault (Romanticism).

Works to look up: Oath of the Horatii, The Third of May 1808, Liberty Leading the People, and Wanderer above the Sea of Fog.

c. 1840–1905REALISM, IMPRESSIONISM & POST-IMPRESSIONISM
Monet · Impression, Sunrise

09 · ORDINARY LIFE, NEW WAYS OF SEEING

The world outside the academy

Realist painters turned toward work, cities and everyday life. Impressionists explored changing light and modern leisure with visible brushwork; Post-Impressionists pushed colour, structure and emotion in different directions. There wasn't one shared style: each artist made a different wager on what painting could do.

Artists: Gustave Courbet, Édouard Manet, Claude Monet, Berthe Morisot, Edgar Degas, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Paul Cézanne, Vincent van Gogh, Paul Gauguin, and Georges Seurat.

Works to look up: The Stone Breakers, Impression, Sunrise, The Cradle, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte, and Starry Night.

c. 1850–1947INDIAN MODERNISMS
Raja Ravi Varma · Galaxy of Musicians

10 · ART IN A CHANGING INDIA

Modernity doesn't arrive in one style

Artists in India responded to colonial institutions, print culture, photography, nationalism and changing urban life in different ways. Raja Ravi Varma combined European academic techniques with Indian subjects; the Bengal School looked to other visual traditions; Amrita Sher-Gil brought modernist approaches to Indian subjects and lived experience. Later artists would challenge all of these models in turn.

Artists: Raja Ravi Varma, Abanindranath Tagore, Amrita Sher-Gil, Jamini Roy, and Nandalal Bose.

Works to look up: Galaxy of Musicians, Bharat Mata, Three Girls, and Jamini Roy's village and folk-inspired paintings.

c. 1900–1945MODERNISM
Picasso · Guernica

11 · BREAK THE PICTURE APART

One world, many viewpoints

Modernists challenged inherited rules about perspective, representation and what a work of art could be. Cubism showed several viewpoints at once; Expressionism distorted form to intensify feeling; Dada and Surrealism questioned reason and ordinary reality; abstraction made line, shape and colour carry more of the work.

Artists: Pablo Picasso, Georges Braque, Henri Matisse, Wassily Kandinsky, Käthe Kollwitz, Marcel Duchamp, Salvador Dalí, and Frida Kahlo.

Works to look up: Guernica, The Dance, Composition VII, Fountain, and The Two Fridas.

c. 1940s–1970sABSTRACT EXPRESSIONISM & POP ART
Rothko · colour as an encounter

12 · GESTURE, COLOUR & MASS CULTURE

Feeling, then looking again

Some post-war artists treated the canvas as a place for scale, gesture and emotional intensity. Mark Rothko layered soft-edged fields of colour; Jackson Pollock worked with poured and dripped paint; Pop artists such as Andy Warhol borrowed the language of advertising and mass production.

Artists: Mark Rothko, Jackson Pollock, Lee Krasner, Willem de Kooning, Andy Warhol, and Roy Lichtenstein.

Works to look up: Rothko's Seagram Murals, Pollock's Autumn Rhythm, and Warhol's Marilyn Diptych.

c. 1970s–todayCONTEMPORARY ART
Anish Kapoor · Cloud Gate

13 · THE QUESTION KEEPS CHANGING

What can art be now?

Contemporary art includes painting and sculpture, but also installation, performance, photography, video, digital work, public interventions and forms that resist easy categorisation. Artists may work with identity, memory, ecology, technology, conflict or the everyday. There is no single contemporary style.

Artists: Anish Kapoor, Yayoi Kusama, Bharti Kher, Nalini Malani, Ai Weiwei, and Jean-Michel Basquiat.

Works to look up: Cloud Gate, Kusama's Infinity Mirror Rooms, Nalini Malani's video/shadow installations, and Bharti Kher's bindi works.

A MAP, NOT A RULEBOOK

Periods overlap. Artists borrow, resist, return and reinvent. The point isn't to memorise every label; it's to have a few doors to open when a painting catches your attention.

02 / COLOUR HAS A BACKSTORY

Every colour
has a passport.

Before a colour becomes a mood, it is matter. A stone dug out of a mountain. A plant grown in a field. A recipe, a trade route, a secret, a danger. Choose a pigment and follow it.

We talk about colour as if it were weightless: blue feels calm, red feels urgent, yellow looks hopeful. These associations can be personal, cultural, religious or political; they are not universal laws. But the material histories of colour are wonderfully concrete. Some pigments were costly enough to signal wealth or devotion. Some travelled thousands of miles. Some were toxic. Some changed the lives of people who never got to choose what they grew.

A quick science note: pigment is the material that gives paint its colour; a dye generally colours a material by dissolving or bonding with it. Historically, artists mixed pigments with binders such as egg, oil, gum or glue. Same colour family, very different chemistry.

01 / THE OLDEST COLOURS

Red: the earth, opened.

Red ochres are among the oldest pigments used by humans. Their colour comes largely from iron oxides in the earth: the same chemistry that stains rocks and soil. Ground into powder and mixed with a binder, earth becomes image. Red can signal blood, danger, life, power, protection or celebration, but its meaning depends on the people using it, and the moment in which it appears.

THE MATERIAL: iron-rich earth, ground and prepared. Not every red pigment is the same: vermilion, for example, is mercury sulfide, a different material with a different risk profile.

A colour begins as a substance before it becomes a symbol.

03 / STAY A MOMENT

The man who said
no to the room.

Mark Rothko is often reduced to a picture of a few big rectangles. But he was trying to make a painting feel less like an object on a wall and more like an encounter you stood inside.

In 1958, Rothko accepted a commission to paint murals for the Four Seasons restaurant in New York's Seagram Building: a very expensive room where wealthy people would dine beneath them. He made a whole group of dark, saturated works. Then he began to doubt the arrangement. He didn't want the paintings to become luxurious wallpaper for people eating dinner. He withdrew from the project rather than let the setting decide what the paintings were for.

I find that story easier to hold than the idea that abstract art has a secret code. Imagine spending months making something, finally getting the prestigious commission, and then realising the work would be seen in a way you didn't believe in. You would have to decide what mattered more: the opportunity, or the experience you wanted the work to create.

Rothko's later chapel paintings took that question somewhere quieter. Fourteen large canvases made a room where visitors could sit, stay, and experience colour without needing to agree on a single explanation. You don't have to feel overwhelmed or enlightened. Start smaller: which edge catches your eye? Does one colour seem to move forward while another recedes? What happens if you look for twenty seconds longer?

04 / HISTORY UNDER YOUR FEET

Bombay is a
living archive.

A city is easier to see when you know what used to be here. Sometimes the clue is a name; sometimes it's a wall that was pulled down, a tram route that vanished, or a building that survived after the world around it changed.

I first started noticing these little entry points into Bombay's past through Anand Kothari's historical notes. They made me look at a familiar street as evidence rather than background: what stood here before this building, why does this neighbourhood have this name, and what disappeared to make the present city possible?

Take the Fort area. It was once enclosed by defensive walls, gates and a moat. During the 1860s, Governor Sir Bartle Frere's administration removed the fortifications as the commercial city expanded; the space they occupied helped make room for a new urban centre, including the grand public buildings we now associate with South Mumbai. The name Fort stayed long after the walls were gone.

Or take the trams. Horse-drawn trams began running in Bombay in 1874, with routes linking places such as Colaba, Crawford Market, Bori Bunder and Pydhonie. Electric trams followed in the early twentieth century; the city's last tram service ended in 1964. The rails are no longer a way to travel through town, but old photographs can make you imagine how different the street once moved.

That is the bit I love about local history: it changes what an ordinary walk can contain. The façades in Fort and Ballard Estate, the old commercial streets around Crawford Market, Oval Maidan's institutional skyline, a remnant of St George Fort on P. D'Mello Road—all are invitations to ask what is still visible, what has been altered, and what is missing.

05 / A RECENT MEMORY · MUMBAI

The painting I took
everyone to see.

A small note of thanks to Rukshaan Art, Sana Krishna, and the kind of conversation that makes you look twice.

I went to Rukshaan Art for The Garden Makers and found myself standing in front of Suresh Pushpangathan's Verdant Symphony. It is a square of green that seems to keep unfolding the longer you look: feathery foliage, blue strokes that flicker through the leaves, white blooms, small bursts of red and yellow. The painting feels less like a garden viewed from the outside than a place you have wandered into and temporarily lost your bearings.

I was taken by the colours in particular. The red and yellow kept catching my eye, although I still can't quite explain why. They seemed to punctuate the greens rather than simply decorate them. I looked, moved away, looked again. Other works in the exhibition had their own material surprises and quiet intricacies, including pieces by artists such as Ashlesha Ravaria. There was so much to notice that I ended up bringing my dad and younger brother back to see it too. I wanted them to have their own encounter with the work.

One thing I loved was that the paintings weren't accompanied by little instruction cards telling me what to think. I could meet the work first, without a ready-made interpretation hovering beside it. I like looking, observing, making my own guesses. I like the possibility that someone else might see something entirely different.

Then I had a lovely conversation with Sana Krishna. She told me stories about some of the pieces, the materials, the decisions and the processes behind them. The paintings became richer, not because my first reading had been wrong, but because I had discovered more doors into the work. I could still keep my own interpretation; now I had a few more things to look for.

That is a generous thing to do for a viewer: not to close the meaning down, but to open it up. I left thinking about the people behind the work, the time and thought held inside a surface, and how much can sit inside a colour you thought you already understood.

So, a little thank-you to Sana and the team at Rukshaan Art for the conversation, the warmth, and for making space for artists and their many ways of seeing. I loved hearing about the gallery's story and its relationship with artists, including those connected with Baroda. If you are in Mumbai and want to spend time with contemporary art, do visit, and give yourself time to look before you read.

A POCKET EXERCISE

Go outside.
Collect evidence.

Pick one colour on your way through the day. Not the most beautiful one. Just the first one you notice. Follow it for five minutes.

01

Photograph it where you found it. Don't tidy the frame.

02

Write down what it is made of, if you can guess: stone, plastic, paint, fabric, light.

03

Ask who chose that colour, who made the thing, and who is meant to notice it.

04

Now look again. Did it change, or did your attention?

06 / MY LITTLE DRAWER

Made for fun.
Filed by year.

I have never made art with an explanation waiting behind it. I like mixed media. I like digital art. I like making something because I want to see what happens if I try. There isn't a grand meaning to any of it. Yet, anyway.

This is not an artist statement. It's a record of things I made across different versions of myself: finished pieces, experiments, digital sketches, things I might have forgotten I made. The year is enough context. The work can speak, or not. It doesn't owe us a thesis.

A LAST LITTLE THOUGHT

Some things are made
to be understood.
Some things are made
to be looked at.

And some things stay with you before you know why. A red stroke in a green garden. A blue that travelled across continents. A face painted five hundred years ago. A building you pass every week. Perhaps history is not only what we know about the past. It is what the past leaves in our hands, and what we decide to notice.
