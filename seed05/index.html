BEFORE WE BEGIN

History was
the curriculum.
The images stayed.

I did rather well in social studies, which is funny because, at the time, I was mostly trying to get through the syllabus. What stayed wasn't a neat sequence of dates. It was the scale of it all: Rome expanding and cracking under its own weight; Greek gods behaving like the most dysfunctional family imaginable; wars, revolutions, empires, cities built to outlast the people who commissioned them.

And then there were the things you could see. A marble hand. A carved doorway. Hieroglyphs that had waited centuries to be read. A palace wall crowded with tiny figures. The strange feeling that a building in Fort or Ballard Estate was still holding on to an earlier Bombay, even as traffic and people moved around it.

Looking back, I think the fascination was never just with what happened. It was with the evidence that something happened: the material left behind, the image that survived, the little detail that outlived its explanation.

What all is hidden from us? A few pages can compress decades into a paragraph. The people who mixed the paint, carried the stone, copied the manuscript, cleaned the studio, paid for the commission, or made a brilliant thing that history forgot often disappear from the frame. One day, the AI revolution will be a paragraph in a school textbook, too. Imagine the lives that paragraph will flatten.

This is not a complete history of art. It is an attempt to look more closely: at the famous names, the overlooked hands, the colours themselves, and the stories that gather around an image long after it is made.

01 / THE MOVING PICTURE

Art history is not
a parade of geniuses.

It is workshops, patrons, arguments, borrowed ideas, trade routes, anonymous labour, new tools, old beliefs—and people trying to make a picture that says what ordinary language cannot.

DRAG YOUR EYES THROUGH TIME

These eras overlap. India was never waiting for Europe to invent art; Europe was never the only place asking ambitious questions of image and material. This is a timeline for orientation, not a ranking.

c. 40,000 BCE onwardBEFORE THE CANON
A mark that outlived its maker

01 — THE FIRST ARCHIVES

Before anyone called it art

There are handprints on rock, animals moving across cave walls, figures in the shelters of Bhimbetka. We can describe the marks; we cannot always know the exact thought that made them. Were they ritual, memory, instruction, play, a way of being together? The honest answer is that the image survives more clearly than its original explanation.

That uncertainty is part of the wonder. Art history begins not with a single civilisation or a European master, but with people making marks that mattered enough to leave behind. Later, at Ajanta, painters would build entire worlds across cave walls—courtly, devotional, crowded with gesture and feeling. The urge to make a world visible keeps changing its tools. It does not belong to one place.

c. 3000 BCE – 500 CEANCIENT WORLDS
The body as public argument

02 — GODS, EMPIRES & AFTERLIVES

Make power visible

Egyptian tomb paintings prepared the dead for another world. Greek sculptors explored idealised bodies and proportion. Roman portrait busts could preserve a face, but they could also make a political argument: this ruler is steady, descended from greatness, fit to lead. Mosaics turned floors into stories; triumphal arches turned victory into architecture.

Across the subcontinent, the Mauryan pillars, Sanchi's Buddhist monuments, early Buddhist sculpture and the painted caves of Ajanta developed other ways to make belief public. A stupa is not a Greek temple in different clothes. The forms arise from distinct rituals, materials and ideas about how people meet the sacred.

And who paid? Who quarried the stone? Who painted the ceiling while a ruler's name went on the building? The surviving monument can make power look singular. Its making was anything but.

c. 500–1400THE SACRED & THE SENSORY
Ajanta · pigment, plaster, devotion

03 — LIGHT IS A MATERIAL

Art was never only on canvas

In medieval Europe, illuminated manuscripts, stained glass, mosaics and carved portals made sacred stories visible to people who might never read a book. Light entered a cathedral through coloured glass and changed the architecture from within. A wall was not a neutral surface; it was part of the experience.

At Ajanta, pigments were applied to carefully prepared surfaces, and faces and hands carry a startling sensitivity across centuries. Chola bronzes could be carried in procession, encountered in movement rather than simply viewed at a distance. Jain manuscripts, Pala Buddhist sculpture, temple murals and textiles all asked the viewer to look differently.

To understand a work, it helps to ask what it was made of and how it was encountered. Was it carried? Lit by a lamp? Seen in a procession? Kept in a palace? Folded into a book? The medium is not just the delivery system. It changes the meaning.

c. 1300–1600THE RENAISSANCE
Michelangelo · marble made to feel alive

04 — RENAISSANCE / RE-LOOKING

The world, measured—and made human

“Renaissance” means rebirth, but the story is not that Europe woke up after a thousand years of sleep. It is a story of particular cities, wealthy patrons, classical texts, new experiments and people arguing about what a human being could know and make. Florence's merchants and bankers, including the Medici, helped finance the art and scholarship that made the city a centre of the period.

Leonardo da Vinci treated looking as a form of investigation. He studied water, anatomy, plants, machines, faces—the world as a problem worth observing closely. Michelangelo could make marble seem to hold breath and grief; Raphael organised figures into poised, lucid worlds. But the signature hides the workshop: apprentices, assistants, stonecutters, preparatory drawings, negotiations, deadlines and patrons with opinions.

Perspective made a convincing space on a flat surface. Anatomy gave bodies a new studied presence. Oil painting allowed slow layers, subtle transitions and luminous depth. None of these techniques appeared from nowhere, and none belonged to Europe alone; knowledge moved through translation, travel, trade and exchange. The Renaissance was not one miracle. It was a dense human network of labour and ideas.

And it was not the only Renaissance happening in the world. In South Asia, artistic traditions and courtly cultures developed along their own paths, with their own experiments in image, devotion, science and power. The useful question is not “who was ahead?” but “what did different societies want art to do?”

c. 1500–1800INDIAN COURTLY WORLDS
A whole court, held in a small space

05 — MUGHAL, RAJPUT & DECCANI

Small paintings. Enormous worlds.

Mughal painting grew through workshops where Persianate manuscript traditions met close observation of people, animals, plants and events. Under Akbar, illustrated histories became ambitious collective projects. Artists such as Basawan and Daswanth helped build a visual language for a vast court; later, Ustad Mansur became celebrated for finely observed animals and plants, while Bichitr painted courtly power with astonishing detail.

These were not solitary artists making little masterpieces in isolation. A manuscript could involve calligraphers, paper-makers, pigment grinders, colourists, painters and binders. The image was collaborative, and the hierarchy of names we inherit does not always reflect the labour inside the page.

Rajput and Pahari traditions made other kinds of worlds: Krishna's stories, the moods of music and seasons, lovers separated by distance, landscapes that feel like emotional weather. Deccani painting could be saturated, strange and dreamlike. There is no single thing called “Indian art.” There are many visual languages, each with its own geography, patronage, beliefs and pleasures.

Look closely at a miniature and it refuses to be small. A leaf has its own veins; a textile its own pattern; a face in the background may be carrying a whole story. The scale asks for a different kind of attention.

c. 1600–1750BAROQUE
Caravaggio · light as a dramatic event

06 — BAROQUE / THE THEATRE OF LIGHT

Make the viewer feel it.

Where Renaissance compositions often sought balance, Baroque art could feel as if the scene had erupted in front of you. Caravaggio's light cuts through darkness like an accusation. Bernini's marble bodies twist, strain and seem to breathe. Rubens fills the canvas with motion. The goal was not merely to show an event but to make the viewer feel caught inside it.

This drama had a history. Catholic reform, religious competition, royal courts and the growing use of art as persuasion shaped the period. Churches and rulers commissioned images capable of moving people emotionally as well as instructing them. Art was devotion, theatre and public messaging at once.

Artemisia Gentileschi deserves more than a sentence as “a female Baroque painter.” She built a serious career in a world that offered women fewer routes into artistic training and authority. Her versions of biblical heroines—especially Judith—have a physical force that changes the temperature of the story. Her biography has often been used to frame her work; it matters, but it should not swallow her artistic decisions, skill or ambition.

Baroque is not just “dramatic art.” It is a reminder that composition can direct the body of the viewer: where you look, what you fear, whose suffering you notice, whose power appears inevitable.

c. 1715–1780ROCOCO
Fragonard · pleasure in motion

07 — ROCOCO / THE ART OF PLEASURE

Silk, flirtation & a world at play

Rococo arrived with curving lines, pastel colours, ornamental interiors, playful mythology and scenes of aristocratic leisure. Fragonard's The Swing turns a garden into a secret joke: a young woman kicks her shoe into the air while a hidden admirer looks up from the bushes. It is charming, mischievous—and a little revealing about who had the leisure to play while others made that leisure possible.

It is tempting to call Rococo merely pretty, as though beauty were a shallow subject. But style carries social information. The room, the silk, the garden and the bodies at play belong to a world of class privilege and courtly performance. Soon, the French Revolution would make that world look politically fragile. Art does not cause history on its own, but it can show us what a society finds normal, desirable, or worth defending.

And the revolution in taste never runs neatly. Rococo overlaps with Neoclassicism; Baroque habits continue; artists borrow and resist. Period labels are useful drawers, not airtight boxes.

c. 1750–1900REVOLUTION / MODERN LIFE
Manet · the present becomes the subject

08 — FROM THE SALON TO THE STREET

Who decided what counted as art?

The French Revolution rearranged political life; industrialisation changed cities, work, transport and time. The art world changed too. Academies and official Salons still held enormous power, deciding what was respectable enough to exhibit. Artists who refused the rules could be ridiculed, excluded or remembered later as revolutionaries.

Édouard Manet painted modern Paris with a bluntness that unsettled viewers. Olympia did not offer the safely distant goddess audiences expected; she looked back. Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe placed a nude woman among clothed modern men, exposing the conventions that allowed some bodies to be idealised and others to be judged. His loose, unblended patches of colour looked unfinished to some critics. They became part of the language of modern painting.

Then Monet and the Impressionists took the changing effects of light seriously enough to make them the subject. In 1874, Monet's Impression, Sunrise gave a critic the word “Impressionism”—first as a sneer, then as the name of a movement. Monet returned to haystacks, poplars, Rouen Cathedral and water lilies, painting the same motif in different light. The subject was partly the world and partly the act of seeing it change.

Berthe Morisot, Mary Cassatt, Camille Pissarro, Alfred Sisley, Frédéric Bazille and others belong in this story too. The canon has often made a few men look like the whole movement; the actual scene was a network of friendships, arguments, shared exhibitions, money problems and women navigating social restrictions to make work.

c. 1850–1947INDIAN MODERNISMS
Many visual languages, one contested modernity

09 — INDIA, LOOKING BACK AND FORWARD

Modernity does not arrive in one style.

Raja Ravi Varma combined oil painting and European academic naturalism with stories drawn from Indian epics and mythology. His images travelled widely through prints, helping shape how generations imagined familiar characters. But Indian modern art did not simply adopt Europe and stop there.

The Bengal School, associated with Abanindranath Tagore and others, searched for visual languages that could resist colonial academic dominance. Jamini Roy drew from folk and popular traditions, simplifying form and using bold, flat colour. Rabindranath Tagore began painting late in life and made expressive, often uncanny images. Nandalal Bose and the Santiniketan circle developed different ideas about art, education, craft and nationhood.

Amrita Sher-Gil moved between Europe and India, studying Western painting and then looking deeply at Indian miniatures, Ajanta and the lives around her. Her palette of ochres, reds, greens and browns is not merely “Indian colour”; it carries an artist's encounter with place, bodies and the limits of representation. Later, the Progressive Artists' Group in Bombay—among them F. N. Souza, M. F. Husain, S. H. Raza, K. H. Ara and H. A. Gade—pushed against inherited rules in a newly independent country.

Art here is not an appendix to European movements. It is a field of argument about colonialism, identity, religion, class, nationhood and what it means to make something modern without surrendering the past.

1940s onwardABSTRACTION / AFTER THE IMAGE
Rothko · colour as an encounter

10 — WHAT IF THE COLOUR IS THE SUBJECT?

Nothing to decode. Nothing to hurry.

Mark Rothko's paintings can look deceptively simple in reproduction: fields of colour, soft edges, rectangles that seem to hover. In person, scale, pigment, surface and the viewer's own body change the experience. Rothko wanted the work to meet a viewer directly; people often describe standing in front of it as meditative, solemn, or unexpectedly emotional. There is no single correct response to have.

Colour-field painting asks a question that runs through this entire archive: does an image need to tell a story in order to mean something? Sometimes the meaning is not a symbol to solve but a feeling that arrives before language. That does not make interpretation irrelevant; it makes attention part of the work.

In India, modern and contemporary artists have also used colour as more than description: S. H. Raza built abstract worlds from geometry, memory and the bindu; V. S. Gaitonde's quiet, layered surfaces invite slow looking; Krishen Khanna has returned to human vulnerability, crowds, labour and the violence of history. Different practices, different questions, no single universal key.

THE NEXT CHAPTER IS STILL BEING WRITTEN

We tend to tell art history backwards, as though each movement knew what it was leading to. Nobody painting in 1874 knew what the textbook would call them. The people making things now don't know what will survive, either.

02 / COLOUR HAS A BACKSTORY

Every colour
has a passport.

Before a colour becomes a mood, it is matter. A stone dug out of a mountain. A plant grown in a field. A recipe, a trade route, a secret, a danger. Choose a pigment and follow it.

We talk about colour as if it were weightless: blue feels calm, red feels urgent, yellow looks hopeful. These associations can be personal, cultural, religious or political; they are not universal laws. But the material histories of colour are wonderfully concrete. Some pigments were costly enough to signal wealth or devotion. Some travelled thousands of miles. Some were toxic. Some changed the lives of people who never got to choose what they grew.

A quick science note: pigment is the material that gives paint its colour; a dye generally colours a material by dissolving or bonding with it. Historically, artists mixed pigments with binders such as egg, oil, gum or glue. Same colour family, very different chemistry.

01 / THE OLDEST COLOURS

Red: the earth, opened.

Red ochres are among the oldest pigments used by humans. Their colour comes largely from iron oxides in the earth: the same chemistry that stains rocks and soil. Ground into powder and mixed with a binder, earth becomes image. Red can signal blood, danger, life, power, protection or celebration—but its meaning depends on the people using it, and the moment in which it appears.

THE MATERIAL: iron-rich earth, ground and prepared. Not every red pigment is the same: vermilion, for example, is mercury sulfide, a different material with a different risk profile.

A colour begins as a substance before it becomes a symbol.

TAP A COLOUR. IT HAS A STORY TO TELL.

01 / BLUE

The colour that travelled from a mountain.

Natural ultramarine was made from lapis lazuli, a vivid blue stone mined historically in what is now Afghanistan. The stone was crushed, washed and processed to separate the blue particles—a labour-intensive process that helped make the pigment extraordinarily expensive in parts of Europe. In some commissions, contracts specified who could use ultramarine and how much. A blue cloak could therefore tell you something about devotion, wealth and the person paying for the painting.

But blue has other histories. In South Asia, indigo dye came from plants and became a major commodity in global trade. Under colonial rule, many cultivators faced coercive arrangements and pressure to grow indigo rather than food crops; the consequences helped provoke the Indigo Revolt in Bengal in 1859–60. A blue cloth can carry beauty, chemistry, commerce and violence at once. The colour is lovely. The supply chain was not always so.

02 / YELLOW

Sunshine, gold, and the trouble with certainty.

Yellow has never meant only happiness. It can evoke sunlight, harvest, sacred radiance, caution, sickness, treachery or wealth, depending on the culture and context. Artists made yellows from earths, plant-derived lakes, lead compounds and other materials. Indian painting traditions used yellow in richly varied ways, while gold leaf could make a surface catch real light rather than merely depict it.

One notorious pigment story is Indian yellow, historically associated with a vivid yellow pigment used in European painting. Older accounts claimed it was made from the urine of cattle fed mango leaves, but that story is disputed and the precise historical production is not securely established. This is a good reminder that art history itself has myths: a story repeated often enough can begin to look like a fact.

03 / GREEN

When a beautiful colour was a bad idea.

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, vivid arsenic-containing greens—including Scheele's green and emerald green—became fashionable in paint, wallpaper, textiles and household objects. They were brilliant, modern and dangerous. The story is more complicated than “green wallpaper killed everyone”: exposure varied, and popular claims about particular deaths are often contested. But arsenic pigments did create genuine occupational and domestic hazards.

It is a story I find especially strange after studying heavy-metal poisoning in FMT. The same material can be an aesthetic breakthrough and a toxic exposure, depending on who handles it, how it is used and what is known at the time. Art conservation is partly the science of learning what beautiful objects are made of—and what that means for the people caring for them.

04 / WHITE & BLACK

The colours that pretend to be nothing.

Lead white was valued for its opacity and handling qualities for centuries, even though lead is toxic. Artists and workshop assistants could be exposed through preparation and use; historical practices varied, and it would be careless to claim every painter routinely poisoned themselves by licking brushes. Some artists did shape a point with their lips, and some materials were hazardous enough to make that a terrible habit. The details matter as much as the dramatic headline.

Black, meanwhile, might come from charcoal, soot, bone black or other sources, each with its own texture and depth. In a painting, black can define an edge, swallow a room, make a colour glow or give a face its weight. White can describe light—or, as with lead white, be a carefully engineered material. Neither is truly “nothing.”

03 / STAY A MOMENT

What if you don't
have to understand it?

I like the idea of standing in front of a Rothko and letting the painting do what it does before rushing to explain it. People describe these colour fields as meditative, even overwhelming; other people feel nothing much at all. Both are allowed. The scale, the edges, the way colour sits on the surface, the room around it and the viewer's own mood all change the encounter.

Perhaps this is the part of art I like most: a work can stay with you without giving you a neat answer. You can leave with a colour lodged in your head and only later wonder why. Not every response needs to be translated into a thesis. Sometimes looking is already a form of attention.

04 / HISTORY UNDER YOUR FEET

Bombay is a
living archive.

Sometimes I like to wander through Fort or Ballard Estate and look up. The old city is not a museum sealed behind glass. It is still working, sweating, selling, commuting, changing—and carrying the marks of who built it and who it was built for.

Follow the tram lines that remain in the city's memory even after the trams themselves disappeared. Look at the arcades, stone façades, carved details and grand institutional buildings. Bombay's colonial architecture can be beautiful and still be part of a history of unequal power; appreciating a building does not require forgetting the system that produced it.

And not every historic trace is monumental. A street name, an old market, a baug, a dock gate, a cemetery, a fountain or a building that has been repurposed can tell a different kind of story. The city is full of small evidence, if you know to look.

05 / A RECENT MEMORY · MUMBAI

The painting I took
everyone to see.

A small note of thanks to Rukshaan Art, Sana Krishna, and the kind of conversation that makes you look twice.

I went to Rukshaan Art for The Garden Makers and found myself standing in front of Suresh Pushpangathan's Verdant Symphony. It is a square of green that seems to keep unfolding the longer you look: feathery foliage, blue strokes that flicker through the leaves, white blooms, small bursts of red and yellow. The painting feels less like a garden viewed from the outside than a place you have wandered into and temporarily lost your bearings.

I was taken by the colours in particular. The red and yellow kept catching my eye, although I still can't quite explain why. They seemed to punctuate the greens rather than simply decorate them. I looked, moved away, looked again. Other works in the exhibition had their own material surprises and quiet intricacies, including pieces by artists such as Ashlesha Ravaria. There was so much to notice that I ended up bringing my dad and younger brother back to see it too. I wanted them to have their own encounter with the work.

One thing I loved was that the paintings weren't accompanied by little instruction cards telling me what to think. I could meet the work first, without a ready-made interpretation hovering beside it. I like looking, observing, making my own guesses. I like the possibility that someone else might see something entirely different.

Then I had a lovely conversation with Sana Krishna. She told me stories about some of the pieces, the materials, the decisions and the processes behind them. The paintings became richer, not because my first reading had been wrong, but because I had discovered more doors into the work. I could still keep my own interpretation; now I had a few more things to look for.

That is a generous thing to do for a viewer: not to close the meaning down, but to open it up. I left thinking about the people behind the work, the time and thought held inside a surface, and how much can sit inside a colour you thought you already understood.

So, a little thank-you to Sana and the team at Rukshaan Art for the conversation, the warmth, and for making space for artists and their many ways of seeing. I loved hearing about the gallery's story and its relationship with artists, including those connected with Baroda. If you are in Mumbai and want to spend time with contemporary art, do visit—and give yourself time to look before you read.

06 / MY LITTLE DRAWER

Made for fun.
Filed by year.

I have never made art with an explanation waiting behind it. I like mixed media. I like digital art. I like making something because I want to see what happens if I try. There isn't a grand meaning to any of it. Yet, anyway.

This is not an artist statement. It's a record of things I made across different versions of myself: finished pieces, experiments, digital sketches, things I might have forgotten I made. The year is enough context. The work can speak—or not. It doesn't owe us a thesis.

A LAST LITTLE THOUGHT

Some things are made
to be understood.
Some things are made
to be looked at.

And some things stay with you before you know why. A red stroke in a green garden. A blue that travelled across continents. A face painted five hundred years ago. A building you pass every week. Perhaps history is not only what we know about the past. It is what the past leaves in our hands—and what we decide to notice.
