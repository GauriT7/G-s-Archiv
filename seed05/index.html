A NOTE BEFORE WE BEGIN

History was a subject.
The images stayed.

I did rather well in social studies, which is funny in retrospect. At the time, history was a curriculum: dates, rulers, wars, revolutions, empires rising and falling in chapters that ended neatly before the bell.

But what stayed with me were the images. Roman ruins. Greek myths. Sculptures with missing noses. Hieroglyphs that had outlived the people who made them. Architecture that seemed to be keeping a secret.

Years later, the dots begin connecting. The question I keep returning to is not only what happened? It is: what has been left out? What disappears when centuries of ordinary lives, forgotten makers, techniques, arguments and accidents are compressed into a paragraph? Someday, even this AI revolution will be a little box in a textbook. Imagine everything that box will leave out.

This is not a complete history of art. It is a trail of things I want to look at more closely.

01 / A VERY INCOMPLETE TIMELINE

People have always
made images.

Move through time. Stop wherever something catches your eye. These dates overlap; art history was never one tidy parade marching in a single direction.

c. 40,000 BCE onward
✋𓃒EARTH / OCHRE / CHARCOAL

BEFORE THE CANON

Marks on a wall

Before art academies, before the word “artist” meant what it does now, people left handprints, animals, signs and stories on rock. In India, the rock shelters of Bhimbetka hold paintings made across long stretches of time. At Ajanta, centuries later, artists painted worlds of movement, devotion and courtly life onto cave walls.

These are not merely the first pages of a European story. They are evidence that image-making emerged in many places, for many reasons: memory, ritual, instruction, pleasure, belief.

c. 3000 BCE – 500 CE
✳
STONE / MARBLE / MOSAIC

ANCIENT WORLDS

Gods, emperors & the afterlife

Egyptian tomb paintings, Greek sculpture, Roman mosaics and portrait busts were not just decoration. They made authority visible, honoured the dead, told sacred stories and helped people imagine their place in a universe that was often frighteningly large.

Greek artists pursued idealised bodies; Roman patrons also wanted likeness, status and public memory. In the Indian subcontinent, the Mauryan pillars, Buddhist stupas at Sanchi, and the early centuries of Ajanta developed their own visual languages. Similar human questions; different answers.

c. 500–1400
⌂
GOLD / STONE / DEVOTION

THE SACRED & THE SENSORY

Art was never only on canvas

Across medieval Europe, illuminated manuscripts, stained glass, mosaics and church sculpture turned buildings into elaborate teaching tools and theatres of light. Across South Asia, temple architecture, bronze icons, murals, textiles and manuscripts carried stories into public space and ritual.

Think of Chola bronzes: metal shaped into figures that could be carried in procession, seen from every angle, and encountered beyond the sanctum. Or the painted worlds of Jain manuscripts and the Pala tradition. The medium was part of the meaning.

LOOK FOR: light passing through colour
c. 1300–1600
PERSPECTIVE / PATRONAGE

EUROPEAN RENAISSANCE

The world, measured again

In parts of Europe, artists experimented with perspective, anatomy, light and the illusion of space. Workshops were busy places: artists, apprentices, assistants and patrons all shaped what survives under one famous name. Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael and Titian became emblematic, but their worlds depended on networks of makers and commissioners.

Meanwhile, the Renaissance was not a universal dawn after a universal darkness. Other regions had their own intellectual, artistic and scientific traditions, flourishing on different timelines. “Renaissance” is a useful label; it is not the whole map.

LOOK FOR: the hands behind the master
c. 1500–1800
PAPER / GOLD / MINERAL COLOUR

MUGHAL, RAJPUT & DECCANI COURTS

Small paintings, enormous worlds

Mughal ateliers combined Persianate manuscript traditions with close observation of people, animals, plants and events. Under Akbar, workshops produced ambitious illustrated histories; later artists developed finely observed portraits and natural-history studies. Artists such as Basawan, Daswanth, Ustad Mansur and Bichitr deserve to be more than footnotes.

Rajput and Pahari painting traditions built other worlds: devotional scenes, courtly life, seasons, music and longing. Deccani painting brought its own saturated colours, dreamlike spaces and distinctive courtly imagination. “Indian art” is not one style, any more than “European art” is.

LOOK FOR: a tiny bird in the margin
c. 1600–1800
DRAMA / SHADOW / THEATRE

BAROQUE & ROCOCO

Make it impossible to ignore

Baroque art often turned light into theatre: Caravaggio's sharp contrasts, Artemisia Gentileschi's fierce heroines, Bernini's marble that seems to breathe, Rubens's movement and grandeur. Art could persuade, overwhelm, delight, instruct and advertise the power of the people who paid for it.

Then Rococo brought ornate rooms, playful elegance and aristocratic pleasure. These styles are not simply “dramatic” and “pretty”; they sit inside histories of religion, court culture, class and political change.

LOOK FOR: where the light lands
c. 1750–1900
REVOLUTION / INDUSTRY / EMPIRE

REVOLUTIONS & NEW WAYS OF SEEING

The frame starts to shake

The French Revolution, industrialisation, colonial expansion, photography and new markets changed who commissioned images and who could see them. Jacques-Louis David painted political virtue; Goya made violence and absurdity difficult to look away from; later, Courbet insisted that ordinary life could be monumental.

In India, Company paintings documented people, plants, buildings and practices for colonial patrons, often translating local knowledge into categories that suited outsiders. Raja Ravi Varma brought mythological subjects into European academic oil painting; Kalighat painters made vivid, modern images for an urban market. Art was adapting—and arguing—under changing power.

LOOK FOR: who is the intended audience?
c. 1860–1930
COLOUR / PERCEPTION / CITY LIFE

IMPRESSIONISM & AFTER

What if seeing is the subject?

Monet painted light changing over water and haystacks; Berthe Morisot and Mary Cassatt made modern life visible from perspectives that the canon too often sidelines. Manet unsettled expectations of what could be shown. Degas watched bodies at work; Van Gogh made colour and brushstroke carry feeling; Cézanne helped open the door to new ways of building a picture.

In Bengal, Abanindranath Tagore and the wider Santiniketan circle questioned colonial academic taste and looked to Indian, East Asian and other traditions. Amrita Sher-Gil later made a strikingly modern language from European training and Indian subjects. Modernism was not one invention exported from one capital.

LOOK FOR: the brushstroke left visible
1940s onward
INDEPENDENCE / MEMORY / ABSTRACTION

MODERN INDIA & THE WORLD

After the old certainties

India's modern art cannot be reduced to a single school. The Progressive Artists' Group—associated with artists including F. N. Souza, M. F. Husain, S. H. Raza, K. H. Ara, H. A. Gade and later Krishen Khanna—responded to a newly independent country with varied, restless visual languages. Elsewhere, artists such as Amrita Sher-Gil, Jamini Roy, N. S. Bendre, V. S. Gaitonde, Tyeb Mehta, Arpita Singh and Nasreen Mohamedi pushed in different directions.

Krishen Khanna's News of Gandhiji's Death (1948) gathers people around a newspaper beneath a streetlight: grief becomes public, and differences of class, gender and community remain visible within shared shock. Khanna's Partition memories also entered his work. History here is not a distant backdrop. It is something people had to live through.

1940s–now
COLOUR AS AN EXPERIENCE

ABSTRACTION & CONTEMPORARY PRACTICE

What if nothing needs to be depicted?

Mark Rothko's fields of colour invite the viewer to stay rather than decode. In the Rothko Chapel, fourteen murals create an environment for contemplation. But abstraction has many histories: S. H. Raza's bindu and geometric vocabularies, V. S. Gaitonde's quiet, layered surfaces, Nasreen Mohamedi's precise lines and countless contemporary artists working across ceramics, fibres, found objects, digital media, performance and painting.

The story keeps going. It includes people who never entered a textbook, craftspeople whose names were not recorded, artists working outside institutions, and new tools that are still changing what making can mean.

LOOK FOR: what happens when you stay

This timeline is deliberately selective, and its dates are approximate. Traditions overlap, travel, borrow, resist and keep going long after a period label has ended.

02 / COLOUR HAS A BACKSTORY

Before it was a colour,
it was something else.

Tap a swatch. Meet the material, the trade route, the danger, the accident—or the person who made it mean something.

Colour feels immediate, almost instinctive. But the colours we take for granted have travelled through geology, botany, chemistry, commerce and human labour. Some were dug from the ground; others coaxed from plants, insects or manufactured in laboratories. Their histories are not always pretty.

Colour meanings below are associations, not universal laws. Red does not mean the same thing to every culture, century or person.

01

EARTH / BLOOD / POWER

Red: a colour with a pulse

Red ochre, coloured largely by iron oxides, is among the oldest pigments humans have used. It can be ground from the earth itself. Other reds have very different origins: vermilion was made from cinnabar, a mercury sulphide mineral; carmine can be made from cochineal insects. One visual family, several radically different material stories.

In European painting, vermilion could bring brilliance to a robe or a sacred detail; in South Asia, red appears across ritual, textiles, manuscripts and painting, with meanings shaped by context. Red can suggest life, danger, celebration, authority or grief. The colour is never just the colour.

FMT connection: cinnabar contains mercury. Historical pigment recipes could expose artists and workshop workers to toxic substances, depending on preparation and handling.

A NOTE FROM THE FMT DESK: beauty and toxicity have sometimes shared a palette. Historical artists did not always know what was in the materials they handled.

A SMALL EXPERIMENT IN LOOKING

Stay with the colour
for a moment.

Mark Rothko's paintings are often described as meditative, but there is no single correct response to them. Their scale, soft edges and layered fields can make colour feel less like a surface and more like a place you have entered.

The Rothko Chapel in Houston contains fourteen murals made for a space intended for contemplation. You do not have to understand them before you look. You are allowed to sit there and feel something—or feel nothing at all.

04 / A RECENT MEMORY

The painting I took
everyone to see.

I went to Rukshaan Art for its nature exhibition, The Garden Makers, and found myself standing in front of Suresh Pushpangathan's Verdant Symphony, a square of green that seemed to keep unfolding the longer I looked. Ferns, flashes of blue, little bursts of red and yellow; a whole ecosystem of marks, and no single place for the eye to settle.

I liked that the works weren't accompanied by a little instruction manual telling me what I was supposed to see. I could look first, make my own guesses, and let the painting remain a little mysterious.

Then I spoke with Sana Krishna, who shared stories about some of the works, the materials, and the processes behind them. Suddenly I could see more—not because my first reading had been wrong, but because there were more doors into the work than I had realised. I liked that very much: the first encounter belonged to me; the conversation made the room larger.

I liked the exhibition enough to bring my dad and younger brother back to see it. We looked at the works together and had our own reactions. Art, it turns out, is a pretty lovely reason to make a family outing.

Somewhere in the middle of all that, I realised I still couldn't explain why the reds and yellows had stayed with me so insistently. Maybe that's the point. A work can leave a colour lodged in your head before you've found the words for it.

A warm thank-you to Sana and the team at Rukshaan Art for the conversation, the generosity, and the reminder that looking can be a shared activity. If you are in Mumbai and curious about contemporary art, go visit.

05 / THINGS I MADE, JUST BECAUSE

No grand meaning.
Not yet, anyway.

I have never made art with a paragraph of explanation waiting behind it. I make things because it is fun, because I like the process, because I want to see what happens when I mix things together.

Mixed media, digital art, experiments, old obsessions, abandoned ideas. No artist statement. No forced symbolism. Just a record of making things across the years.

A LAST LITTLE THOUGHT

Some things are made
to be understood.
Some things are made
to be looked at.

And sometimes you look, and look again, and the thing stays with you. A colour. A face. A story you didn't know was there. Maybe that's enough to begin with.
