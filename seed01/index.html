AN OPEN INVESTIGATIONSCROLL, WANDER, RETURN
THE STARTING POINT 01—06

On war, and all the places it goes.

I started with Tehran. Or, more accurately, Tehran found its way into my head through a television series, a novel, and a stationery shop I had never visited.

I hadn’t been to the city. I was getting to know it in fragments: a street on a screen, a life unfolding on a page, a past that kept appearing at the edges of the story.

And naturally, I started wondering about everything the stories weren’t showing me.

What does a political event actually change once it leaves the headlines and enters somebody’s everyday life?

The obvious answers came first. Borders. Governments. Economies. The things history books arrange into dates and consequences.

But I kept thinking about the less obvious things. The fabric that becomes scarce. The recipe that changes when an ingredient is no longer available. The painting that looks different once you know what was happening outside the artist’s window. The family that leaves home with a suitcase and spends generations explaining where home used to be.

A war rarely stays in the chapter we give it.

It travels.

And I wanted to follow it.

THREAD 01HISTORY

THINGS LEAVING TRACES

a city, seen
through stories

A screen. A novel. A city too large to fit inside either.

Begin with Tehran.

I first approached Iran through Tehran, the espionage thriller, and Marjan Kamali’s The Stationery Shop of Tehran. Very different stories, both leaving me curious about the place beyond the plot.

The series gives us a Tehran of surveillance, divided loyalties and hurried decisions. The novel takes us back to 1953, where Roya meets Bahman in a neighbourhood stationery shop filled with books, fountain pens, ink and paper. Their young love unfolds against political upheaval, until the events of that year alter the future they had imagined.

The stories are different, but both left me wondering about the lives happening just outside the frame.

The 1953 coup itself is worth pausing over. Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh had moved to nationalise Iran’s oil industry, challenging British oil interests and the geopolitical calculations of Britain and the United States. The coup that removed him became a turning point in modern Iranian history, with consequences that reached far beyond one government.

But I didn’t want to stop at the event itself.

What did Tehran look like before the camera arrived? What did its young people wear? What filled its bookshops and cinemas? What was cooking in its kitchens? What happened to the things people made, believed in and planned to become?

A city is more than the events that make it into history books. It is also its markets, its music, its everyday routines, its arguments, its architecture and the lives people are busy living.

One story had led me to a city. The city was leading me to everything else.

THREAD 02WAR & CONSEQUENCE

POWER & CONSEQUENCE

the things that happen
between the dates

People, unfortunately, do not experience their lives in timelines.

History loves a date. A revolution begins. A government falls. A war ends. We give the event a name, draw a line beneath it and move on to the next chapter.

People don’t get to move on quite so neatly.

In Iran, the 1953 coup changed the country’s political direction. In The Stationery Shop, that history becomes personal: a relationship interrupted, a future that never quite arrives, and questions carried for decades.

The same tension appears in other histories, too. The French Revolution changed ideas about monarchy, citizenship and political power. The American Revolution reshaped the relationship between a government and the people it claimed to represent. The Partition of India in 1947 redrew borders while forcing millions to rebuild their lives elsewhere. The World Wars altered cities, industries, families and the movement of people on a scale still visible today.

These events are not interchangeable. Each has its own causes, people, injustices and consequences. But they invite a shared question: what happens after the moment history remembers?

A political upheaval may take place over days. Its consequences can settle into a family for years.

They appear in the language spoken at home, the relatives who live elsewhere, the stories parents tell their children, and the photographs that acquire a significance nobody thought to give them when they were taken.

A political event changes the conditions of everyday life. Those conditions change the choices available to people. Their choices change what they preserve, what they abandon and what they pass on.

History enters the household.

It rarely knocks first.

THREAD 03FOOD & MEMORY

CULTURE, TRADE & MEMORY

the kitchen
remembers

A recipe can become an archive without ever looking like one.

Follow the thread into food.

Let’s begin with something less imposing than a battlefield.

A kitchen.

Rice steaming beneath a lid. Onions browning in oil. Herbs being chopped, a spice tin opening, somebody asking whether there is enough for one more person.

Food is wonderfully ordinary. It is also a record of where people have been.

Persian cuisine offers a lovely place to start: saffron rice, tart barberries, herbs folded into kuku sabzi, slow-cooked khoresh, and tea poured beside something sweet. At Nowruz, the Persian New Year, sabzi polo and fish are associated with renewal and the arrival of spring.

There are histories of agriculture, trade, climate, celebration and regional identity tucked into these dishes. There are also family habits, personal preferences and the fact that a meal is rarely just about what’s on the plate.

In The Stationery Shop, familiar food helps Roya carry her Iranian identity into a life lived elsewhere. A taste can bring a place closer, even when returning to it is no longer simple.

Which makes me think about a different question: what happens to food when people have to leave?

Consider the Sindhi experience of Partition. In 1947, Sindh became part of Pakistan, and many Hindu Sindhis migrated to India, rebuilding their lives away from the homeland that had shaped their culture.

What does a community carry when it cannot carry home?

Sai bhaji, kadhi, koki, dal pakwan and festive tahiri belong to a culinary inheritance shaped by Sindh’s own geography, agriculture, trade and community life. After migration, familiar dishes could help make a new home feel recognisable. Ingredients might change, methods might adapt, but the meal could still carry a memory of somewhere else.

The story isn’t unique to Sindhi food, although its particular history deserves to be understood on its own terms. Across cultures, cooking can preserve identity through migration, marriage, trade and generations of family life.

And food also tells us about class.

Consider pizza. Its history is rooted in Naples, where flatbreads topped with inexpensive ingredients were part of everyday working-class food. The familiar story that Margherita pizza was created in honour of Queen Margherita in 1889 is widely repeated, though details of that origin story are debated. What is less uncertain is the transformation that followed: a food associated with Naples became an international favourite, appearing in restaurants and fine-dining rooms far removed from its humble beginnings.

Food can travel upwards through class as well as across borders. What one generation eats because it is affordable, another may celebrate as heritage; what begins as ordinary street food can become a carefully plated delicacy.

The same ingredient can tell different stories depending on who is cooking, who is eating and what it costs.

Of course, food is vulnerable to circumstances beyond the kitchen. War disrupts agriculture and supply routes. Scarcity changes what is available. Displacement separates families from ingredients, markets and the people who know how to prepare a dish.

Sometimes a substitute becomes tradition. Sometimes a recipe survives only in memory, or in the way an older relative describes it.

What begins as a question of supply can become a question of belonging.

The kitchen is where history learns to taste like something.

THE NEXT THREAD
THREAD 04ART & FASHION

WHAT WE CHOOSE TO SEE

the wardrobe
is not innocent

A historical document with excellent tailoring.

Follow the thread into fashion.

Clothes can look like the least serious thing in a history book. Until you start asking who made them, what they were made from, who could afford them and what wearing them meant.

Take the corset. Across different periods, it has been treated as an instrument of fashionable shape, a marker of respectability, a product of skilled labour and a symbol in arguments about women’s bodies. Its history is more complicated than a simple story of oppression or liberation. What it meant depended on the person wearing it, the time and place, and the expectations surrounding her.

Or take leather: once a practical material for protection, footwear, saddlery and workwear, it now moves between utility, luxury, subculture and status. The material remains recognisable; its social meaning changes.

Then there’s denim.

Jeans began as hard-wearing work clothing, useful for people whose jobs put their clothes under real strain. In 1873, tailor Jacob Davis and businessman Levi Strauss patented the use of rivets to reinforce the stress points of work trousers. The resulting jeans became associated with durability and labour long before they became a global fashion staple.

Today, denim can signal rebellion, practicality, luxury or simply a Tuesday morning. But its history still carries traces of mining, manufacturing, workers and mass production.

A pair of jeans has a journey: raw material, factory, labour, branding, fashion cycle, wardrobe.

Wartime clothing tells a related story. In Britain during the Second World War, fabric restrictions and utility schemes encouraged economical designs. Then Christian Dior’s 1947 New Look arrived with a defined waist and full skirts that used far more fabric than wartime austerity had encouraged. Its reception was shaped by a world still negotiating scarcity, recovery and the desire for something different.

Fashion doesn’t change for one reason alone. Materials, labour, class, commerce, politics and personal taste all leave their marks on what we wear.

A silhouette can tell us what a society could afford, what it admired, what it was trying to leave behind—or what it was trying to become.

Now look beyond Europe.

Iran's clothing history cannot be reduced to a before-and-after story of revolution. Qajar-era portraits, early twentieth-century photography, changing urban fashions, modernist influences, the politics of dress and the personal choices women make today belong to different moments and different circumstances.

Qajar-era portraits, textiles and photographs reveal changing ideas about beauty, status and self-presentation. Later periods brought new artistic influences and new debates about modernity, public life and dress.

Clothing can be personal preference, social expectation, political statement or a negotiation between what someone wants and what their circumstances permit. Often, it is several of these at once.

When we look at an old photograph, how much of what we see was chosen freely, and how much was shaped by the world in which that person lived?

A wardrobe, it turns out, can be a historical document with excellent tailoring.

✳✳✳

when history enters the frame

Art lets us look at a world through the things people chose to make, preserve, celebrate or challenge.

Begin with Persian miniature painting. During the Safavid period, artists created richly detailed scenes in which architecture, gardens, figures and calligraphic traditions could work together to tell a story. The paintings often drew upon literary works, including the Shahnameh, the great Persian epic.

Persian miniature painting brought together architecture, gardens, figures, colour and calligraphy to tell stories. Many miniatures were connected to literary works, including the Shahnameh, the Persian epic. The image was not merely decoration beside the text; it was another way of telling the story.

Move forward into the Qajar period, and portraiture and photography reveal changing encounters between older artistic traditions and newer ways of representing the world. Later, artists such as Kamal-ol-Molk helped popularise a naturalistic, academic style in Iranian painting.

Art can preserve a tradition. It can also help reform it, challenge it or record a society changing around it. Sculpture, architecture, photography, prints, paintings and posters each tell stories in different ways. Some commemorate rulers and victories; others document ordinary life, advertise new ideas, or ask the viewer to question the world they have inherited.

And posters can do something else entirely: persuade, recruit, rally, warn, sell or resist. Their bold colours, striking typography and compressed messages make them particularly revealing records of what a society wanted people to believe—or do.

And then there is art made in the shadow of war.

Christopher Nevinson’s Returning to the Trenches conveys the relentless, almost mechanical movement of soldiers during the First World War. Käthe Kollwitz’s prints and drawings dwell on grief, hunger, maternal anguish and the human cost of conflict. Otto Dix’s Der Krieg portfolio confronts the violence of war without dressing it up as glory.

Their approaches differ, but they make one thing clear: art is never just a pretty object sitting outside history.

It can glorify war. It can document it. It can challenge the stories told about it. It can give a face to suffering that might otherwise disappear into a statistic.

An image does not give us the whole truth. But it can change the questions we bring to the history.

Sometimes art isn’t answering a question. It’s teaching us how to look.

THE NEXT THREAD
THREAD 05ECONOMICS

WHO GETS WHAT, AND WHY

the price of
ordinary things

Economics becomes tangible when it begins interfering with your day.

Follow the thread into economics.

A price rises. An ingredient disappears from a shelf. A factory changes what it produces. A family postpones a purchase.

Suddenly, the economy isn’t a collection of charts. It is the difference between what somebody planned to buy and what they can afford to bring home.

But economics is not only about prices. It is also about power: who owns the land, the factory, the railway, the resource, the company; who makes the rules; who takes the risk; and who gets the reward.

The American Revolution is often told as a story of independence and political rights. It was also shaped by arguments about taxation, trade, imperial control and who had the authority to decide how an economy should work. Political freedom and economic interests have been entangled for a very long time.

Move forward into America’s industrial age, and another cast of characters appears.

Andrew Carnegie and steel. John D. Rockefeller and oil. Jay Gould and railways. J. P. Morgan and finance. These men built enormous fortunes in an era of rapid industrial growth, fierce competition and vast inequality. Their stories include innovation and organisation, but also ruthless business tactics, concentrated power and battles over labour and regulation.

The question isn’t simply how they became rich. It’s what their wealth allowed them to influence—and what the systems they helped build meant for everybody else.

This is where books like The Tycoons become interesting: they make the growth of an economy feel less like an abstract curve and more like a contest between people, companies, resources and ambition.

Charlie Munger’s mental models encourage looking at decisions through more than one lens: incentives, psychology, competition and the unintended consequences of systems. Ray Dalio’s writing explores how debt, money, economic cycles and power interact over time. Their frameworks differ, but both invite us to look beyond the immediate transaction and ask what larger forces are at work.

These are ways of thinking, not magic formulas. Real economies are messier than any one model, and people do not all begin with the same choices or opportunities.

The everyday connections are everywhere. The price and availability of fabric influence clothing. The movement of people changes labour markets and communities. A disruption in trade can change what reaches a kitchen. The financial circumstances of a household shape the opportunities available to its members.

Economics, at its most human, asks more than What does this cost?

Who made it, who benefits, who bears the cost—and who gets to decide?

THREAD 06CULTURES & PEOPLE

THE ONES CONNECTING IT ALL

people, who were
never footnotes

History is experienced one life at a time.

Follow the thread back to people.

A revolution, a war, a migration or an economic crisis can become so large in our minds that the people within it begin to disappear.

But they weren’t living through a chapter title.

They were getting dressed. Making breakfast. Falling in love. Going to work. Arguing with their families. Waiting for somebody to come home.

They were making plans with the information they had—which was almost certainly less than the information we have now.

This is what draws me to The Stationery Shop. The political history matters, but so does the ordinary life caught up in it. The shop is a place where people meet, exchange ideas, discover books and imagine a future. Paper, ink, letters: objects that seem small until the circumstances around them change.

A photograph, a recipe, a piece of jewellery or a familiar book can become a bridge between the life somebody had and the life they are trying to build.

The object survives. The person changes. The meaning changes with them.

I think about how much of a family's history is held in things that would seem unremarkable to anyone outside it. The dish somebody insists on making a particular way. The story that gets told at every gathering. The place-name that appears in conversation even when nobody in the room has ever been there.

There is a whole history of belonging in these details.

And there is a responsibility in paying attention to them. To remember that a person's life cannot be reduced to the circumstances that interrupted it.

They had a life before the event. They have a life beyond it, too.

PULL BACK, LOOK AGAIN

the web begins
to show itself

We began with Tehran. We followed a story into war, then into food, fashion, art, economics and the lives of people.

None of the threads stayed where we left them.

Food led us to migration, class and memory. Fashion led us to labour, materials and identity. Art led us to the ways people record, celebrate and challenge the worlds around them. Economics brought us back to the household—and then out again, to industries, fortunes and systems of power.

The connections are not always direct. Persian miniatures and wartime posters belong to different traditions. Iranian and Sindhi histories are distinct. A recipe and a railway map seem to have little in common until we ask what they reveal about movement, resources, memory or belonging.

The point isn’t to make everything fit one tidy pattern. It is to notice where the threads genuinely meet, and where the differences matter.

The world isn’t a collection of subjects waiting to be filed into separate drawers.

Perhaps curiosity is about becoming curious enough to notice the connections that are already there.

ONE LAST THREAD

and then,
keep looking.

I started with a television series and a book.

I ended up thinking about a plate of food, a pair of jeans, a painting, a family photograph, a stationery shop, an oil empire and the price of things.

Somewhere along the way, the subjects stopped behaving like subjects. They began speaking to one another.

That’s the part I want to keep exploring: how one story sends you looking for another; how a small detail changes the way you see something familiar; how the things we encounter become part of the way we understand the world.

Curiosity isn’t always about collecting more information. Sometimes it’s about learning to see what is already there a little differently.

The web is unfinished. There are more threads to pull, more histories to read, more things I haven’t noticed yet.

For now, I’ll leave a few of them loose.

After all, the interesting part is what happens when you follow one.
