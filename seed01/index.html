THE STARTING POINT 01—06

On war, and all the places it goes.

There is a peculiar thing about stories. You enter through one door and, before you know it, you're standing somewhere entirely unexpected.

Mine began, in part, with Tehran.

A television series. A novel. A stationery shop. A city I had never walked through, yet one I was beginning to recognise through its streets, its people, its silences.

And then the questions began.

What does a war actually change?

The obvious answers came first. Borders. Governments. Economies. The things we learn in history books, arranged neatly into dates and consequences.

But I kept thinking about the less obvious things.

The dinner that tastes different because an ingredient is suddenly unavailable. The dress that looks the way it does because fabric was once rationed. The painting that makes sense only when you know what happened outside the artist's window. The family that leaves home with a suitcase and spends the next three generations explaining where home used to be.

A war, I was beginning to realise, has a rather inconvenient habit of refusing to stay in its own chapter.

It travels.

And I wanted to follow it.

THREAD 01HISTORY

THINGS LEAVING TRACES

a city, seen
through stories

A screen. A novel. A city too large to fit inside either.

Begin with Tehran.

I first approached Iran through Tehran, the espionage thriller, and Marjan Kamali's The Stationery Shop of Tehran. Two very different windows, opening onto a place that cannot possibly be contained by either.

In the series, Tehran is a city of surveillance, divided loyalties and hurried decisions. The camera moves through its streets with the urgency of someone who cannot afford to stop for too long.

In the novel, we return to Tehran in 1953. Roya finds refuge in a neighbourhood stationery shop, a place of books, fountain pens, ink bottles and paper. There she meets Bahman. Their young love unfolds against a city on the brink of political upheaval, until violence interrupts the future they had imagined together.

One story moves at the speed of a thriller. The other lingers over a life that history has interrupted.

Neither tells us everything about Iran. That, perhaps, is the first thing worth remembering about looking at a country through a screen or a page: the window may be beautifully made, but it is still a window.

Beyond it lies a country of many centuries, languages, landscapes, arguments, ambitions and ordinary afternoons.

And somewhere between the two stories, I began to wonder about the life that continues outside the frame.

What did the city look like before the camera arrived? What did its young people wear? What filled its bookshops and cinemas? What was cooking in its kitchens? What happened to the things people made, the things they believed, and the things they had planned to become?

And what becomes of all these things when the world they belong to changes?

One story had led me to a city. The city was leading me to everything else.

THREAD 02WAR & CONSEQUENCE

POWER & CONSEQUENCE

the things that happen
between the dates

People, unfortunately, do not experience their lives in timelines.

History has a fondness for dates. It likes a beginning, a turning point, a decisive afternoon. It gives us names for events and labels for the periods that follow.

People, unfortunately, do not experience their lives in timelines.

The 1953 coup in Iran belongs to the political history of the country. In The Stationery Shop, it also becomes the interruption of a young couple's future. A meeting that does not happen. A person who does not return. Questions that remain unanswered for decades.

This is what fiction can do that a chronology often cannot: bring us close enough to feel the distance between an event and the life it alters.

A political upheaval may take place over days. Its consequences can settle into a family for years.

They appear in the stories that parents tell their children, the stories they deliberately leave out, the language spoken at home, the relatives who live elsewhere, and the photographs that acquire a significance nobody thought to give them when they were taken.

Sometimes the most revealing part of history is not what happened, but what people had to learn to live with afterwards.

And this is where the web begins to form.

A political event changes the conditions of everyday life. Those conditions change the choices available to people. Their choices change the way they live, what they preserve, what they abandon and what they pass on.

History enters the household.

It rarely knocks first.

THREAD 03FOOD & MEMORY

CULTURE, TRADE & MEMORY

the kitchen
remembers

A recipe can become an archive without ever looking like one.

Follow the thread into food.

Let us begin with something far less imposing than a battlefield.

A kitchen.

A pot on the stove. Rice steaming beneath a lid. The scent of onions browning in oil. A handful of herbs being chopped, a spice tin being opened, somebody asking whether there is enough for one more person.

Food is wonderfully ordinary. It is also a record of where people have been.

Persian cuisine offers a particularly lovely place to explore this. Think of saffron rice, the tartness of barberries, herbs folded into kuku sabzi, the slow, patient cooking of a khoresh, or the ritual of tea poured beside something sweet. Think of the sabzi polo and fish associated with Nowruz, the Persian New Year, when the arrival of spring is marked at the table as much as on the calendar.

There are histories of agriculture, trade, climate, migration and celebration tucked into these dishes. There are also preferences, family habits and the simple fact that a meal is rarely just about what is on the plate.

In The Stationery Shop, food helps Roya carry her Iranian identity into a life lived elsewhere. Familiar flavours become a connection to a home she can no longer inhabit in quite the same way. Cooking becomes one way of holding on, and eventually, of sharing that inheritance with others.

Which makes me think about food in a different context: what happens when people have to leave?

Consider the Sindhi experience of Partition. Sindh itself became part of Pakistan in 1947, and many Hindu Sindhis moved to India, rebuilding their lives without the homeland that had once anchored their culture. The Sindhi experience of displacement is not identical to Iran's history, of course, but it opens a related question: what does a community carry when it cannot carry home?

Sometimes, it carries recipes.

Sindhi sai bhaji, with its greens, lentils and vegetables; kadhi served with rice; koki, rolled out and cooked on a griddle; dal pakwan, with its crisp pakwan and spiced lentils; the sweet, rich comfort of a festive tahiri. These dishes belong to a particular culinary inheritance, shaped by Sindh's own geography, trade, agricultural traditions and community life.

They are not simply food that survived migration. They are ways of remembering a place, of teaching children something about their inheritance, of making a home feel recognisable even when its coordinates have changed.

And the kitchen is not the only place where this happens.

Iranian cuisine has its own histories of regional variation, exchange and migration. Sindhi cuisine has its own. Their stories should not be collapsed into one another; the interesting thing is how each reveals a different way of preserving identity.

Across cultures, a recipe can become an archive without ever looking like one.

No leather binding. No official seal. Just a little of this, a little more of that, and somebody who knows when it is ready.

And yet, even these intimate traditions are vulnerable to circumstances beyond the kitchen. War can disrupt agriculture and supply routes. Scarcity can change what is available. Displacement can separate families from ingredients, markets and the people who know how to prepare a dish.

The food changes. Sometimes the recipe adapts. Sometimes a substitute becomes tradition. Sometimes a dish survives only in memory, or in the way an older relative describes it.

What begins as a question of supply can become a question of belonging.

The kitchen is where history learns to taste like something.

THREAD 04ART & FASHION

WHAT WE CHOOSE TO SEE

the wardrobe
is not innocent

A historical document with excellent tailoring.

Follow the thread into fashion.

Clothes are often treated as the frivolous cousins of history. Lovely to look at, certainly, but surely less consequential than the things happening outside the fitting room.

I am not entirely convinced.

A silhouette can tell us something about the materials available to a society, the work people do, the rules they live under, and the identities they wish to present to the world.

Consider wartime Britain. During the Second World War, fabric restrictions and utility clothing encouraged economy in design. Garments had to make practical use of limited resources. Mending became part of the wardrobe's maintenance rather than a charming hobby one might take up on a Sunday.

Then came Christian Dior's 1947 New Look: rounded shoulders, a defined waist and full skirts that used considerably more fabric than wartime austerity had encouraged. The collection arrived while rationing was still affecting Britain, making its extravagance all the more striking.

The contrast is fascinating. A changing material reality had shaped what could be made; a changing cultural mood helped determine what people wanted to wear next.

The relationship is not a neat equation. Fashion does not change for one reason alone. Designers, manufacturing, class, commerce, politics and personal taste all have a hand in it. But the thread is there.

Scarcity can influence a silhouette. A silhouette can become an ideal. An ideal can tell us something about what a society has been missing.

Now look beyond Europe.

Iran's clothing history cannot be reduced to a before-and-after story of revolution. Qajar-era portraits, early twentieth-century photography, changing urban fashions, modernist influences, the politics of dress and the personal choices women make today belong to different moments and different circumstances.

The Qajar period, for instance, offers a remarkable visual archive of how beauty and identity were represented. Court portraits, textiles and photographs reveal changing ideas of femininity, masculinity, status and self-presentation. Later periods brought new artistic influences and new debates about modernity, public life and dress.

Clothing is never merely what somebody happened to put on that morning. Sometimes it is a personal preference. Sometimes a social expectation. Sometimes a statement. Sometimes a negotiation between what someone wants and what their circumstances permit.

And sometimes it is all of these at once.

Which raises a question I rather like: when we look at an old photograph, how much of what we see was chosen freely, and how much was shaped by the world in which that person lived?

A wardrobe, it turns out, can be a historical document with excellent tailoring.

✳✳✳

when history enters the frame

I keep coming back to the idea that art is both a record of the world and an attempt to make sense of it.

It is also a rather good place to see how differently people have understood the same world across time.

Begin with Persian miniature painting. During the Safavid period, artists created richly detailed scenes in which architecture, gardens, figures and calligraphic traditions could work together to tell a story. The paintings often drew upon literary works, including the Shahnameh, the great Persian epic.

Look closely at one of these miniatures and notice how much the image asks of you. A garden is not simply a garden. The arrangement of figures, the buildings, the colours and the relationship between image and text all contribute to a larger narrative.

Art here is not just an illustration placed beside a story. It is one of the ways the story is told.

Move forward into nineteenth-century Iran, and the visual world changes. Qajar portraiture and the spread of photography reveal new encounters between older artistic traditions and new technologies of representation. Later, artists such as Kamal-ol-Molk helped establish a naturalistic, academic style that changed the direction of modern Iranian painting.

Then modernism enters the picture, bringing new ways of thinking about form, abstraction and the relationship between art and contemporary life. Artists begin to negotiate what it means to be modern without simply abandoning the traditions that came before.

There is no single Iranian art history, any more than there is a single Iranian experience.

And this becomes particularly important when we turn to war.

In Europe, the First World War profoundly altered the visual language of artists who confronted industrialised destruction. Christopher Nevinson's Returning to the Trenches conveys the mechanical, almost relentless movement of soldiers. Käthe Kollwitz's prints and drawings dwell on grief, maternal anguish and the human cost of conflict. Otto Dix's later work, including his Der Krieg portfolio, refuses to make the violence of war beautiful.

These artists did not all respond in the same way. Their work belongs to different artistic practices, experiences and perspectives. But together, they demonstrate something powerful: when the world becomes difficult to comprehend, art can become a way of confronting what ordinary language struggles to hold.

It can glorify war. It can document it. It can challenge the stories told about it. It can preserve the face of a person who might otherwise disappear into a statistic.

And it can make us look again.

There is a difference between knowing that people suffered and standing before an image that asks you to consider what that suffering looked like.

The image does not give you the whole truth. No image can. But it can change the questions you bring to the history.

That is one of the things I love about art: it is not always trying to give us an answer. Sometimes it is teaching us how to look.

THREAD 05ECONOMICS

WHO GETS WHAT, AND WHY

the price of
ordinary things

Economics becomes tangible when it begins interfering with your day.

Follow the thread into economics.

Economics can sound terribly abstract until it begins interfering with your day.

A price rises. An ingredient disappears from a shelf. A factory changes what it produces. A family postpones a purchase. A government decides which goods are available, and in what quantities.

Suddenly, the economy is not a collection of charts. It is the difference between what somebody planned to buy and what they can afford to bring home.

War can redirect labour and industrial production, disrupt trade, damage infrastructure and place pressure on public finances. Those changes travel through supply chains and into household budgets. Their effects are not evenly distributed: some people lose their livelihoods, others find new work, and still others gain from the new conditions.

The point is not that every economic change can be traced to a single cause. It is that decisions made far from the household can alter the choices available within it.

And this is where the earlier threads meet.

The price and availability of fabric influence clothing. The availability of ingredients influences cooking. The movement of people changes labour markets, communities and demand. The financial circumstances of a household shape the opportunities available to its members.

Even art and fashion exist within material realities. Artists need materials, time and somewhere to work. Designers need textiles, skilled labour, manufacturers and customers. Cultural production is creative, but it does not take place outside the world that sustains it.

This is what makes economics interesting to me when it is approached through everyday life.

Not simply who has what, but how the arrangement came to be. Who makes the decisions. Who benefits. Who absorbs the cost. And whose ordinary life changes as a result.

The question what can I buy? can be a very personal one.

It can also be a question about the entire system surrounding you.

THREAD 06CULTURES & PEOPLE

THE ONES CONNECTING IT ALL

people, who were
never footnotes

History is experienced one life at a time.

Follow the thread back to people.

There is a particular kind of distance that history can create.

We learn about a revolution, a war, a migration or an economic crisis, and the event becomes so large that it threatens to swallow the lives within it.

But the people who lived through history were not living through a chapter title.

They were getting dressed. Making breakfast. Falling in love. Going to work. Arguing with their families. Waiting for somebody to come home.

They were making plans with the information they had, which was almost certainly less than the information we have now.

This is what draws me to stories like The Stationery Shop. The political history matters, but the novel also asks us to consider what it means for an ordinary life to be caught up in events beyond its control.

The stationery shop itself becomes more than a setting. It is a place where people meet, exchange ideas, discover books and imagine a future. Its objects are small, but their significance grows with the lives attached to them.

Paper. Ink. Letters. The possibility of sending a message and expecting an answer.

These are ordinary things until the circumstances surrounding them change.

And perhaps this is why personal objects can carry so much weight in histories of migration and displacement. A photograph, a recipe, a piece of jewellery, a familiar book or a handwritten letter can become a bridge between the life somebody had and the life they are trying to build.

The object survives. The person changes. The meaning changes with them.

I think about how much of a family's history is held in things that would seem unremarkable to anyone outside it. The dish somebody insists on making a particular way. The story that gets told at every gathering. The place-name that appears in conversation even when nobody in the room has ever been there.

There is a whole history of belonging in these details.

And there is a responsibility in paying attention to them. To remember that a person's life cannot be reduced to the circumstances that interrupted it.

They had a life before the event. They have a life beyond it, too.

PULL BACK, LOOK AGAIN

the web begins
to show itself

By now, the original question has become a rather different one.

We began with war. We followed it into economics, then into food, fashion, art, media and the lives of people. But none of these threads has remained where we left it.

Food brought us to migration and memory. Fashion brought us to scarcity, labour and identity. Art brought us to the stories societies tell about themselves, and the stories artists choose to challenge. Economics brought us back to the household. Literature brought us close enough to see that history is experienced one life at a time.

And the web continues beyond these connections.

Iranian history and Sindhi history are distinct, but both invite us to consider the relationship between place, displacement and cultural inheritance. A Persian miniature and a wartime poster belong to very different visual traditions, yet both remind us that images are shaped by the worlds in which they are made. A recipe and a handwritten letter seem to have little in common until we ask what happens when the person who knows the recipe, or the person who wrote the letter, can no longer be reached.

The connections are not always obvious. Some are direct; others are comparisons that help us ask better questions. Not every thread leads to the same conclusion, and not every story can be made to fit one tidy pattern.

That is precisely the point.

The world is not a collection of subjects waiting to be filed into separate drawers. It is a set of relationships, constantly shifting, sometimes contradictory, often more interesting than the categories we use to describe it.

Perhaps the task is not to find a connection between absolutely everything.

Perhaps it is to become curious enough to notice the connections that are already there.

ONE LAST THREAD

and then,
keep looking.

I started with a television series and a book.

I ended up thinking about a plate of food, a dress, a painting, a family photograph, a stationery shop and the price of things.

Somewhere along the way, the subjects stopped behaving like subjects. They began speaking to one another.

And perhaps that is what I have been trying to understand all along: how the things we encounter become part of the way we see the world. How a story sends us looking for another story. How a detail, once noticed, changes the meaning of everything around it.

I don't think curiosity is always about collecting more information. Sometimes it is about learning to see the information we already have differently.

The web is still unfinished. There are more threads to pull, more histories to read, more things I haven't noticed yet.

For now, I think I'll leave a few of them loose.

After all, the interesting part is what happens when you follow one.
