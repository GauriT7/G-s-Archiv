There is a particular kind of person who keeps going.

Not always because they are fearless. Sometimes because they have learned what to do with fear.

Sometimes because the next point is the only thing in the world they need to think about.

— g.

A NOTE FROM THE SIDELINES

what happens
when you keep going?

I have always been fascinated by athletes. Not just the obvious things—the speed, the strength, the impossible-looking shots, the photographs taken at the exact second someone appears to be flying. I am interested in the person underneath all that spectacle.

Their private rules. The routines that look absurd until you understand what they might mean. The years of repetition that nobody applauds. The particular loneliness of having to make a decision, in real time, with your body and reputation on the line.

Perhaps this began before I had words for it. My parents were busy, so my brother and I were sent towards sports—any sport, all sports, preferably the kind that kept us moving. The shadow of that childhood remains: I still gravitate towards movement, towards the company of people doing something difficult together, towards the delicious exhaustion that can make a good night's sleep feel like a small miracle.

But I am also interested in the longer question. If we are fortunate enough to have decades ahead of us, how do we look after the body and mind that will have to live through them? What does it mean to be ambitious about a life without treating yourself as a machine that can be run at full power indefinitely?

For clues, I keep looking at people who have made a study of the limits.

The scoreboard is only one version of the story.

FIELD NOTE 01THE CHILD ON WHEELS

fall down.
get back on.

I was six or seven when I got into roller skating. At one point, I was apparently among the better skaters in the city—not bragging, just reporting what may be the most efficient medal-earning strategy of my childhood.

What I remember most is the speed. Racing someone much older than you and realising, with a little shock, that you might actually win. The wind, the wheels, the narrow strip of time when everything is happening too quickly for overthinking to catch up.

And then there is the other part. You fall. You bleed. Someone checks that you're alright. You get up, find your place, and go back onto the track. It sounds neat when written as a metaphor, but at the time it was mostly a scraped knee and the stubborn desire not to be done yet.

I still wear my inliners now and then. There is something lovely about returning to a sport that knew you before you knew very much about yourself. The body remembers things the mind has filed away: how to balance, how to lean, how to trust a little momentum.

I don't think every difficult thing needs to become a lesson. Sometimes a sport is just beautiful. Sometimes the lesson arrives years later, when you realise that the feeling of falling and continuing has quietly followed you elsewhere.

FIELD NOTE 02RITUALS & ROUTINES

little things,
precisely.

Watch Rafael Nadal before a serve. The bottles are aligned just so. There are familiar gestures, a careful sequence, a small choreography that repeats itself between points. To an outsider, it can look like superstition dressed as administration. To a player, it may be a way to return to the same small patch of ground, mentally, before stepping into uncertainty again.

Professional sport is a strange place to be a human being. There is a crowd, a clock, an opponent trying to undo you, and a result that may be replayed in slow motion by millions. You cannot control the bounce of the ball, the wind, or the moment your nerves decide to make themselves known. You can control how you prepare for the next point.

Rituals can offer structure and reduce the number of decisions a person has to make under pressure. That does not mean every athlete's habit is a secret performance hack, or that we can know exactly what a gesture means to its owner. Sometimes a routine is practical; sometimes it is personal; sometimes it is simply a routine that has stuck.

Across sports, the forms change. A basketball player has a familiar free-throw sequence. A tennis player bounces the ball before serving. A swimmer moves through the same warm-up before stepping onto the blocks. A footballer might take the same breath before a penalty. The details differ; the small return to the present is the thread.

I think about this outside sport, too. The glass of water before an exam. The song you play before a hard conversation. The five-minute tidy before you can begin working. We tend to dismiss these things as quirks, when sometimes they are little bridges between feeling overwhelmed and being ready to act.

When pressure narrows the world to one point, one shot, one decision, perhaps a ritual says: you have been here before. You know what comes next. Begin there.

FIELD NOTE 03ENDURANCE

the long way
through.

There is a moment in an endurance event when the original excitement has been used up. The start-line energy is gone. The crowd becomes background noise. The body has begun sending its very clear feedback. What remains is not the dramatic version of motivation, but a more ordinary negotiation: the next kilometre, the next sip, the next turn of the pedals.

Triathlon is almost comically good at making this visible. A wetsuit tugging at your shoulders; the disorienting churn of an open-water start; the effort of finding your line while other swimmers kick and splash around you. Then the transition: wet body, bike shoes, helmet, a bicycle engineered to waste as little energy as possible. Nutrition becomes strategy. Carbohydrates become logistics. The race is a series of small problems wearing one very long outfit.

And then, eventually, there is the run. Legs that have already done their share are asked to keep negotiating. Training for this is not one heroic act but a long accumulation of ordinary sessions—many of them repetitive, some tedious, all of them building a capacity that cannot be downloaded the night before.

When an Ironman athlete crosses the line, the emotion can be difficult to separate into neat categories. Relief, pride, exhaustion, disbelief, gratitude. The finish line makes the effort visible, but the feeling belongs to all the mornings that led there.

Nirmal “Nimsdai” Purja's Project Possible offers a different, more extreme version of this question. After a career in the British military, including service with the Gurkhas and Special Boat Service, he set out to climb all fourteen of the world's 8,000-metre peaks. He completed the project in 2019 in a little over six months. The scale of the feat is astonishing; so are the preparation, risk, logistics and accumulated experience behind it.

What interests me is not the fantasy that a person can become invincible. No mountain grants that. It is the way a chosen challenge can rearrange your sense of what is possible—and the care required to remember that courage and recklessness are not synonyms. Nature is not a motivational prop. It has its own terms, and it does not owe anyone a happy ending.

Perhaps the deepest attraction is the encounter with a limit. You reach the edge of what you thought you could do, and discover that the edge was not quite where you expected. Sometimes you go farther. Sometimes the wiser achievement is knowing when not to.

FIELD NOTE 04THE COURT

alone on
the court.

Tennis is an especially demanding kind of theatre. It asks for speed, stamina, coordination, tactical intelligence and the ability to recover emotionally between points. One bad decision can be followed immediately by another opportunity to make a better one. The match keeps moving; the mind must learn to move with it.

I don't compete now, but my younger brother does, and I've seen what training asks of him. The repetition. The heat. The hours in which improvement is so gradual that it can be hard to tell whether anything is happening at all. There is the player, the coach, the physio, the family, the schedule, the travel and the match itself. From the outside, a tournament is a few days on a calendar. From the inside, it is built on a life arranged around practice.

In singles, there is no teammate to take the next shot for you. Your team may be in the stands, but the decisions arrive on your side of the net. You must manage your attention, your body and your response to the last point while the next one is already approaching. It is a peculiar kind of solitude: supported by many, alone at the moment of execution.

India's tennis ecosystem also makes the journey harder than the highlight reels suggest. Access to courts, quality coaching, competition, travel and sustained funding can shape who gets to stay in the sport long enough to develop. Talent matters, but so do the structures that allow talent to survive the years before anyone is paying attention. In a country where sport is still often treated as a riskier career than a conventional degree, choosing it can mean asking a family to believe in a future that is far from guaranteed.

And the players we know best—the ones whose names become shorthand for greatness—have lived inside this machinery for decades. Novak Djokovic, Rafael Nadal and Roger Federer are not one story repeated three times. Their games, temperaments and approaches differ. What they share is the extraordinary length of time they spent learning to compete at the very highest level, under the gaze of a world that can turn a loss into a verdict on a person's worth.

That may be why I find the private rituals so compelling. They are small, human things in the middle of a spectacle. A player can be a global name and still need to settle their breathing, reset their feet, or remind themselves to play the next point rather than the whole match at once.

FIELD NOTE 05AFTER THE APPLAUSE

when the game
ends.

There is a part of athletic greatness that we rarely know how to watch: the ending.

For years, the athlete's life has been organised around the next match, the next season, the next recovery block. Their body is not merely how they get around the world; it is the instrument through which they work, compete, earn, and understand themselves. Their calendar has a rhythm. Their people have roles. Their purpose has a shape.

Then the body changes. The results change. A sport that once felt like an open road becomes a negotiation with pain, time, or the knowledge that someone younger is arriving. Retirement can be a choice, a relief, a grief, or several of these in the same afternoon.

Roger Federer's farewell at the 2022 Laver Cup remains an unusually visible example of that transition. After a career spanning more than two decades, he played his final professional match alongside Rafael Nadal in doubles. The photographs of them sitting together, both in tears, travelled far beyond tennis. They captured something that statistics cannot: the end of a shared era, and the tenderness that can exist between people who have spent years trying to defeat one another.

Federer had spent much of his adult life being Roger Federer, the player. The next chapter required a different kind of competence: learning to live without the calendar that had once organised everything. For any athlete, the question after sport is not simply “What now?” It is also “Which parts of me remain when the thing that shaped my days is gone?”

There is a quiet warning here for the rest of us. We often make a single pursuit carry the weight of our entire identity: the degree, the company, the project, the body we are trying to build. It can give our days direction. It can also make change feel like disappearance.

Perhaps a life well lived needs both devotion and other rooms to walk into when one door closes. A person can love a thing completely without asking it to be the only thing that makes them who they are.

FIELD NOTE 06THE QUANTIFIED SELF

the body
keeps notes.

I wear a WHOOP, and I am not particularly troubled by the data. Mostly, I am curious. What is my body doing? How is it recovering? What happens if I change one thing? Is there a pattern here that I have been too busy to notice?

Sometimes the answer is practical. Sleep becomes less negotiable. Routine stops feeling like a punishment and starts looking like a way to keep the lights on. I have become more aware of when I eat, how much I rest, and how easily a life lived in permanent flight-or-fight mode can begin to feel normal simply because it is familiar.

One of the stranger discoveries has been how early my body sometimes seems to signal that I am getting sick. Before a fever fully arrives, I have noticed changes in resting heart rate, temperature trends and respiratory rate. On the days when illness takes over, the data can look dramatically different from my usual baseline. A wearable cannot diagnose an infection, and these measurements can shift for many reasons—but as a personal nudge to pay attention, it has been hard to ignore.

It has also made me gentler with the idea of recovery. I can try to do too much, burn through my energy, and call it ambition. Or I can stack the quieter things: enough sleep, regular meals, movement, pauses, a routine that leaves some charge in the battery. I want to be around for the future I keep working towards. It would be rather counterproductive to arrive there completely depleted.

There is a comic side effect, too. I have become the sort of person who notices everything. The cleanliness of a surface. The mystery of a public chair. Whether something has touched something else it absolutely did not need to touch. My clean sheets are a protected ecosystem; if the conditions are not right, I will not be entering. Sanitiser has become less an object and more a recurring character in my life.

My family may reasonably have notes on this. I am not claiming that every extra precaution is rational, or that a wearable has turned me into a perfectly regulated person. I am simply more conscious of my choices—and occasionally insufferable about them. The body, it turns out, is not the only thing collecting data. So is my brain, and it has a very active quality-control department.

That is the useful side of tracking, for me: not obedience to a score, but a better conversation with myself. The number is a clue, not a command. The goal is not to optimise every hour. It is to understand enough to live more of them well.

FIELD NOTE 07OPTIMISATION CULTURE

the shortcut
question.

There is a whole industry devoted to becoming a better version of yourself, preferably by next Thursday. Protein targets, supplements, recovery scores, body-composition goals, expensive equipment, expensive advice. Some of it is useful. Some of it is harmless fun. Some of it has acquired the tone of a moral obligation: if you are not improving, what exactly are you doing with your life?

I participate in this culture. I track, read, experiment with routines, and like understanding how the body works. I also find it unsettling when the pursuit of health starts to look like a competition in which the winner is the person who can afford the most interventions—or tolerate the most risk.

There is a difference between training to become stronger and believing that your body is a problem to be solved. There is a difference between a medical treatment used for a clear indication, under appropriate supervision, and treating prescription drugs or research compounds as casual lifestyle accessories. Newer weight-loss medicines, including drugs in the GLP-1 family, have changed clinical care for many people. That does not make every use appropriate, and it does not turn unapproved substances into harmless experiments.

The same goes for performance-enhancing drugs. It is not difficult to see why someone might be drawn to a faster route: visible change can bring attention, confidence, opportunity, or a sense of control. But steroids and other substances carry real risks, and the online version of the story rarely gives equal time to the cost. You do not need to look jacked to be healthy. You do not need to make your body a public project to prove you take care of it.

I don't have a neat verdict on every form of enhancement. Medicine itself is, in many ways, the careful use of interventions to change what the body can do. Context matters: the evidence, the indication, the risks, the alternatives, the person making the decision, and whether they understand what they are choosing. Curiosity is good. Pretending that uncertainty has disappeared because a product is trending is not.

And then there is the larger contradiction: we are surrounded by conveniences designed to reduce effort, while paying for elaborate ways to reintroduce effort into our lives. We sit all day and buy a class that makes us sweat. We outsource friction, then book a race to practise enduring it. We want comfort and challenge, sometimes in the same afternoon.

I keep thinking about the Roman phrase panem et circenses—“bread and circuses”—used by the poet Juvenal as a critique of a public kept distracted by provisions and spectacle. The phrase has a particular political history; it is not a tidy explanation of the modern fitness industry. But it leaves me with a question: what do we do with our appetite for spectacle, now that the arena can be a gym, a feed, a finish line, or a perfectly lit transformation video?

Perhaps chosen discomfort can be a kind of freedom. It asks us to participate in our own lives rather than remain passive consumers of ease. But it can also become another product, another performance, another way to feel inadequate. The distinction is not always obvious. I am still looking at it.

AN OPEN QUESTION

When does self-improvement help you inhabit your life—and when does it persuade you that your life, as it is, is not enough?

LAST NOTE / NOT A CONCLUSION

play the
long game.

I think what I admire most about athletes is not that they can endure more than the rest of us. It is that they show, in a very visible way, what can happen when a person keeps returning to something: when the work is repeated, the limits are tested, the body is listened to, and the mind learns to begin again.

I want to carry some of that into the rest of my life. The patience to be a beginner. The humility to train. The ability to take a bad day as information rather than a final verdict. The discipline to keep promises to myself, and the sense to change the plan when the plan stops serving me.

There are so many things I want to do. It is mildly offensive that a day still contains only twenty-four hours, a year only 365 days, and a life no guaranteed number of either. I want to learn, travel, make things, build things, see what happens if I follow a question far enough. Some of it will take longer than I would like. Some of it will have to wait. Life, inconveniently, does not promise to keep the schedule open.

But I am learning to think less in terms of doing everything immediately and more in terms of staying capable of doing things for a long time. To build layers of good, gentle effort. To take rest seriously. To let ambition be expansive without letting it become an emergency.

Maybe the point was never to win every race. Maybe it is to keep enough wonder, health, and room in yourself to want to enter another one.

I think I'm trying to prove that the things I want are possible for me. Not all at once. Not on someone else's clock. But, with time, I can get there—and still be here to enjoy it.
