THE MEMORY TRIGGER

TEXTURE IS NOT A FOOTNOTE

The bite has a soundtrack.

A crisp fracture, a wafer's brittle layers, a soft cookie centre, the way chocolate melts: structure changes how a food feels and even how it sounds. Crispness and crunch can be studied through mechanical tests and acoustic recordings. Moisture, porosity and the size of air pockets all matter.

CONCEPTUAL CURVE · NOT A UNIVERSAL RECIPE

THE BLISS POINT

Can a flavour be tuned?

The term bliss point, popularised by food scientist Howard Moskowitz, describes an especially appealing level of sweetness in a product. The useful idea is that formulations can be tested systematically. The oversimplification is that there is one magic sugar-salt-fat ratio for everybody. Preferences vary by person, product and context.

There is a difference between making a food enjoyable, making it consistent and making it something people buy again. Food companies study all three.

THE CASE FILES

Same job. Different kinds
of delicious.

Different brands have built recognisable sensory signatures. These are not secret recipes; they're little windows into the engineering choices that make a product itself.

01 / LAYS

The fragile crunch

Potato variety, dry matter and reducing sugars influence colour and frying behaviour. Thin slicing, rinsing, drying and controlled frying produce a crisp, low-moisture structure. Oil and seasoning then shape aroma, flavour release and mouthfeel. A chip has to fracture cleanly, not chew like a potato.

POTATO → SLICE → FRY → SEASON → SEAL
02 / PRINGLES

The identical curve

Unlike a conventional whole-potato chip, Pringles are made from a dough based on processed potato ingredients, shaped into a consistent saddle-like form and fried. That controlled geometry makes the crisps stack neatly and behave predictably in the tube. The package is part of the product's engineering, not an afterthought.

FORMULATE → SHEET → CUT → SHAPE → FRY → STACK
03 / CHEETOS

The airy, cheesy puff

Extrusion cooks and expands a corn-based mixture as it exits a die and pressure drops. The porous structure creates a distinctive light crunch. A coating of oil helps seasoning adhere; particle size and surface properties affect how the flavour lands.

CORN MIX → EXTRUDE → EXPAND → DRY → COAT
04 / KITKAT

The layered snap

Thin wafer sheets and chocolate create alternating crisp and smooth layers. The contrast is the point: a brittle bite against a coating that softens quickly in the mouth. Chocolate tempering gives the coating its gloss and snap; the wafer contributes the light fracture.

WAFER LAYERS + TEMPERED CHOCOLATE
05 / LINDT

The smooth melt

Particle size, cocoa butter, formulation and conching all shape chocolate texture. Conching continuously mixes and aerates warm chocolate, helping refine flavour and mouthfeel. Tempering then encourages the desired cocoa-butter crystal structure. The result: gloss, snap and a smooth melt.

ROAST → GRIND → CONCH → TEMPER → MOULD
06 / TOBLERONE
△△△

A shape you can recognise blindfolded

Honey-almond nougat gives Toblerone its chewy contrast against milk chocolate. Its triangular peaks create a distinctive silhouette and a recognisable break. The name itself combines Tobler and torrone, the Italian word for nougat. A product identity built into the geometry.

CHOCOLATE + NOUGAT + A VERY GOOD SHAPE
07 / MORE TO BITE INTO

Other clever little systems

Oreo: crisp biscuit, sweet cream and a carefully controlled snap. Pop Rocks: carbon dioxide trapped in a hard candy matrix, released as it dissolves. Ice cream: a controlled mix of ice crystals, air cells, fat and sugars; the size of those structures changes whether it feels creamy or icy. Jelly and gummies: hydrocolloids such as gelatin or pectin form networks that determine chew, bounce and melt. Carbonated drinks: dissolved CO₂ creates the fizz and bite, while acidity and aroma balance sweetness.

08 / COCA-COLA
coca
cola

It is never just
the drink.

Cola is a carefully balanced beverage: sweetener, acidity, carbonation, colour and a complex aroma profile. Carbon dioxide gives the fizz and trigeminal bite; acids provide contrast; flavour compounds make the drink recognisable. The formulation has to remain consistent whether it is poured from a can, a bottle or a fountain.

There is a whole portfolio built around different preferences, too. In India, Coca-Cola sells the classic version, Diet Coke and Coca-Cola Zero Sugar. The zero-sugar products use high-intensity sweeteners rather than sugar, but Diet Coke and Coke Zero are not identical formulations. A zero-sugar label doesn't turn every dietary question into a solved one, either; it changes the sweetener and energy profile.

And then there is distribution. This is where I find Coca-Cola particularly impressive. You can be in a small town, a busy city, a roadside shop or an airport and still recognise the red branding, the fridge, the board above the counter or the familiar bottle. It is not an accident, nor is it simply a clever advertisement.

The company makes concentrates and syrups; its independent bottling partners mix, bottle and package the drinks, then work with distributors, wholesalers and local retailers to get them to the last point of sale. The Coca-Cola Company does not own or control most of those local bottlers. Its 2025 annual report says the system reaches more than 200 countries and territories and delivers about 2.2 billion servings a day. That is an extraordinary physical network, not just brand recall.

I remember watching a podcast interview with one of the company's executives and being struck by how much of the business was about getting the product to the last point of sale. The exact interview is one I'll add to my listening notes; the idea stayed with me. A brand can be famous everywhere, but it has to be available everywhere, too.

And it is not always one can with lunch. I have seen large bottles become an everyday fridge staple, poured glass after glass; when a soft drink is consumed by the litre, it can start to occupy the slot that plain water would otherwise fill. That is a habit worth noticing, not a hydration recommendation. Sugared versions add free sugars; zero-sugar versions change the formulation, but water still deserves to be the default drink.

03 / A LITTLE GEOGRAPHY

Where a cuisine
begins.

A crop is never just a crop. It is often the beginning of a much bigger story.

The availability of water, soil characteristics, temperature, growing seasons and cultivation practices influence which crops can be produced efficiently in a region. Over time, these crops become central to local food systems, markets and culinary traditions.

And once a crop becomes a staple, it can shape far more than the contents of a kitchen.

It can influence livelihoods, labour patterns, trade, taxation, political priorities and the wider economy.

WHEAT

Flour, fermentation
& the everyday loaf.

Wheat becomes flour; flour meets water; yeast or a sourdough culture produces gas and flavour; gluten creates the elastic network that traps it. Fermentation is both a biological process and a flavour-making technique. Bread is one of the simplest ways to see agriculture, microbiology and chemistry working together.

COFFEE

Bean, roast,
and a little ritual.

Coffee is a seed from a fruit. Processing, fermentation, drying, roasting and grind size all influence the cup. Roast chemistry creates aroma compounds; brewing extracts them into water. Climate, altitude, shade, cultivar and post-harvest methods shape the raw material before the barista ever arrives.

TEA

One plant.
Many personalities.

Black, green, white and oolong teas all come from Camellia sinensis. Differences in withering, oxidation, rolling, heating and drying create very different aromas, colours and tastes. A small processing decision can change the character of the leaf.

VANILLA

A flower, a pod,
a long cure.

Vanilla comes from orchids. The pods are harvested before full ripeness and cured through a lengthy process involving heat, drying and conditioning. Enzymatic changes develop vanillin and many other aroma compounds. It is labour-intensive, which helps explain why genuine vanilla can be expensive.

THE POTATO

Domesticated in the Andes, it travelled to Europe through Spanish colonial networks. In Ireland, heavy dependence on potatoes made the blight-driven Great Famine of the 1840s catastrophic. A crop can underpin food security; crop failure can expose how fragile that security is.

THE CHILLI

Chillies originated in the Americas and spread into Asia through post-Columbian trade. They became deeply embedded in many Indian regional cuisines. Something can arrive by ship and, over generations, feel entirely local.

THE SPICE ROUTES

Pepper, cinnamon, cloves and cardamom travelled through networks that linked the Indian Ocean to Europe and West Asia. The demand for spices helped shape maritime expansion, commercial competition and colonial power. A pinch of pepper has a long paper trail.

BREAD & POWER

Grain prices and bread availability have repeatedly mattered in political history. In pre-revolutionary France, the cost and scarcity of bread contributed to public anger. Food is everyday infrastructure; when it fails, politics arrives at the table.

05 / THE COCOA STORY

This one is
my favourite.

A tropical fruit, fermentation, a global supply chain, and an ingredient that may soon be made in a rather different way.

I think chocolate is the perfect subject for this whole seed. It is something so familiar that most of us barely think about where it comes from. And yet, the journey from a cacao pod to a chocolate bar brings together botany, microbiology, chemistry, engineering, trade and economics.

Once I started reading about cultivated cocoa, I found myself looking backwards: first to the lab, then to the tree, then to the farms and the people who grow it. The chocolate bar had become a map.

THE ECONOMY BEHIND THE BAR

Who grows it?
Who earns from it?

Cocoa production is concentrated in tropical regions, particularly West Africa. Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana are among the world's most important producers. Yet growing the bean and selling the finished chocolate are very different positions in the value chain.

Farmers cultivate, harvest and ferment. Traders, processors and manufacturers handle other stages. Branding, formulation, distribution and retail can capture substantial value further downstream. The price of a bar does not flow neatly back to the farm.

The sector also faces difficult questions around farmer incomes, child labour, deforestation, disease, ageing plantations and climate stress. The ingredient is delicious; the supply chain is not simple.

06 / THE FOOD LABORATORY

Engineering
the edible.

This is the part that sent me down the rabbit hole. Not just making food differently, but understanding how carefully its texture, sound, melt, aroma and flavour are designed.

Food engineering is not only about replacing ingredients or producing food in a bioreactor. It is also the science of making a cookie spread just enough, a wafer break cleanly, chocolate melt at the right moment and a snack sound exactly as crisp as it feels.

Some of the most interesting food experiments happen in restaurants, too. A tasting menu can play with temperature, texture, aroma, memory, shape and expectation. A familiar ingredient arrives in an unfamiliar form, and suddenly the meal becomes a small lesson in sensory science.

01 / DAIRY PROTEINS

Whey & casein

Precision fermentation can use microorganisms to produce dairy-identical proteins such as whey and casein without conventional dairy farming. The proteins can then be used in foods, subject to formulation and regulatory requirements.

02 / ALTERNATIVE FATS

Designed to melt

Researchers are exploring fats with specific nutritional, functional and melting properties: the right solid-fat profile, mouthfeel, stability and behaviour during cooking or chocolate production.

03 / CULTIVATED MEAT

Cells, not carcasses

Animal cells are grown in controlled environments and encouraged to form tissue. The ambition is to produce meat without raising and slaughtering an entire animal. Scale, cost, structure and regulation remain major challenges.

04 / ALTERNATIVE PROTEINS

More than plants

Plant proteins, fungi, mycoprotein, microbial biomass and other sources are being developed as ingredients for new foods. Their functionality matters as much as their protein content: solubility, flavour, texture and processing performance.

05 / FOOD ENZYMES

Biology at work

Microorganisms can produce enzymes used in baking, dairy processing, brewing and other manufacturing. Enzymes help control reactions, improve consistency and create desired textures or flavours.

06 / FLAVOUR COMPOUNDS

Making the familiar

Fermentation can produce specific flavour ingredients, including compounds associated with familiar foods. The goal may be to reproduce a note, improve consistency or create a new flavour building block.

FIG. 04 / THE TEMPERED BAR

A CLOSER LOOK / CHOCOLATE

The snap, the gloss,
the melt.

Cocoa butter can crystallise into several forms. Tempering is the careful heating and cooling process used to encourage the desirable crystal form, giving chocolate its gloss, clean snap and stable texture. Conching is different: it mixes and aerates warm chocolate, helping refine flavour and mouthfeel by reducing harsh notes and improving particle distribution.

MELT→COOL→REHEAT→MOULD

And then comes the moment you eat it. The chocolate softens close to body temperature, the fat phase melts, and its finely dispersed particles create that smooth sensation. Tiny changes in particle size, fat composition and temperature can make a surprisingly large difference.

THE ENGINEERING OF A PERFECT BITE

Not just flavour. The whole experience.

FLAVOUR+TEXTURE+SOUND+AROMA+TEMPERATURE+EXPECTATION

A cookie's spread depends on fat, sugar, flour, water, leavening and baking temperature. Its centre can stay soft while the edges set and brown. The melt-in-the-mouth quality depends on fat melting behaviour, particle size, moisture and structure. The snap of a wafer depends on its brittle layered structure. The sound of a crisp is generated as its cellular structure fractures; researchers can record acoustic emissions and relate them to perceived crispness. A product can be measured with texture analysers, microphones, rheometers and carefully designed sensory panels.

Sweetness, saltiness, acidity, fat and umami are not isolated dials. They interact with one another and with aroma and texture. A little acid can lift sweetness; salt can bring out other flavours; glutamate and nucleotides add savoury depth; fat changes how aromas are released and how long a flavour lingers. The aim is not simply to add more. It is to create balance.

THE IDEA THAT STARTED THIS SEEDLAB NOTE 006-A

Do we need to grow
the original crop?

Cocoa production faces several pressures: climate change, plant disease, ageing plantations, productivity constraints, deforestation and labour concerns. Cocoa trees grow within a relatively narrow band of tropical conditions, and production is concentrated in a small number of regions. This creates vulnerabilities in a supply chain that feeds a very large global chocolate market.

So companies are exploring alternative ways to produce cocoa ingredients. One example is Celleste Bio, which is developing cocoa ingredients through plant-cell culture. Mondelēz International, the company behind Cadbury, Toblerone and other major brands, has invested in the venture.

The idea is to cultivate cacao cells in controlled conditions and produce cocoa-derived ingredients without depending entirely on conventional bean cultivation. It challenges a basic assumption: perhaps we do not always need to grow the whole crop in order to make the ingredient we want.

It is a compelling possibility, but not a solved problem. Scale, cost, energy requirements, regulatory approval, consumer acceptance and the sensory behaviour of the resulting ingredients all matter. A laboratory demonstration and an ingredient that can reliably supply a global chocolate brand are two very different milestones.

I remember being fascinated by the idea that the companies behind the chocolate I grew up eating were investing in a future where some of its ingredients might be produced in a lab. It was the moment this seed began taking shape. I had started with a bar of chocolate and ended up thinking about biotechnology, farming, climate and the future of food.

07 / A STORY I REMEMBER

Seven little
seeds.

A school lesson, a coffee story, and the strange feeling of being transported by a few lines on a page.

I remember a chapter from my English textbook at school called Coorg. It was in First Flight, and I can still picture the page. The hills, the rainforests, the coffee plantations tucked under tree canopies, the air that the chapter described as breathing of invigorating coffee. It made Coorg feel less like a place in a textbook and more like somewhere I had briefly been allowed to visit.

The chapter stayed with me in a very particular way. I remember reading it and feeling almost as though I was there: the rolling hills, the smell of coffee, the mist, the forests. I could practically imagine walking through an estate. It is funny what the mind decides to keep from school. Sometimes it is not the answer to the question at the end of the chapter. It is the feeling of being somewhere else for a few minutes.

Years later, while reading about coffee and food science, I came across the story of Baba Budan and the seven seeds again. And suddenly that old chapter returned. It felt like a small thread connecting the person I was when I read it to the person I am now, still following stories about food.

MOCHA → BABA BUDAN GIRI

THE COFFEE LEGEND / C. 1600

Seven seeds, carried
across a sea.

According to the familiar legend, Baba Budan travelled through Yemen and encountered coffee in Mocha. The story says he concealed seven viable seeds against his body, smuggled them out and carried them back to India, planting them in the hills now associated with Baba Budan Giri in Karnataka. The image is wonderfully cinematic: seven tiny seeds, hidden on one traveller, crossing a border and eventually becoming part of a country's agricultural story.

It is a wonderful story, but the precise date and details belong to a historical tradition rather than a surviving contemporary travel diary. The Coffee Board of India repeats the account of seven Mocha seeds planted around 1600; commercial plantations developed much later, during the eighteenth century. A legend, followed by an industry.

Seven seeds. A rather determined traveller. And an ingredient that would become part of the landscape, economy and everyday rituals of a country.

08 / THE BODY KEEPS SCORE

Food in.
Signals out.

The biology after the bite: glucose, insulin, digestion, metabolism and the long view of dietary patterns.

Food doesn't stop being interesting once it reaches the stomach. What happens next is a coordinated series of digestive, hormonal and metabolic processes. The body breaks food down, absorbs nutrients and directs them towards energy production, storage, repair and other functions.

It is also why a conversation about food cannot end with calories or cravings. Nutrient quality, fibre, processing, meal patterns, physical activity, sleep and individual biology all influence metabolic health.

INSULIN RESISTANCE

The signal gets harder to hear.

Target tissues respond less effectively to insulin. The pancreas may compensate by producing more; in susceptible people, this can progress towards prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Persistently high glucose can, over time, damage blood vessels and raise the risk of cardiovascular, kidney, retinal and nerve complications. Risk is shaped by genetics, adiposity, activity, sleep, diet and other factors.

GLYCATION & AGEs

When sugars meet proteins.

Glycation is a non-enzymatic reaction between sugars and proteins or lipids. Over time, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) can accumulate and contribute to tissue changes, particularly in chronic hyperglycaemia. This is not the same as saying that eating sugar directly “ages” every tissue; the biology is more specific.

PCOS & METABOLIC HEALTH

Hormones do not work alone.

PCOS is associated with insulin resistance in many, though not all, people. Higher insulin levels can contribute to increased ovarian androgen production and altered reproductive signalling. The 2023 international guideline recommends the oral glucose tolerance test as the most accurate way to assess glycaemic status in PCOS, regardless of BMI. Metabolic risk deserves attention alongside menstrual and reproductive symptoms; PCOS does not look the same in everyone.

FIBRE & THE GUT

Not just roughage.

Dietary fibre influences bowel function, satiety, glucose absorption and the gut microbiota. Some fibres are fermented by gut microbes, producing short-chain fatty acids. Pulses, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds are useful food sources.

A NOTE ON PROCESSED FOOD

Processing isn't one big villain.

Freezing, pasteurising, fermenting, milling and preserving can improve safety, shelf life and access. The concern is not that a food has been touched by a machine; it is the nutritional pattern and the properties of certain industrial formulations. Some ultra-processed foods are high in free sugars, sodium or energy density and low in fibre. Frequent reliance on these foods can displace more nutrient-dense options. Context, quantity and the overall dietary pattern matter.

The WHO recommends keeping free sugars below 10% of total energy intake, with a further reduction towards 5% potentially offering additional benefit. This is a population-level recommendation, not a scorecard for one meal.

AND THE MONTHLY CYCLE?

Cravings can have a calendar, too.

Appetite and food preferences can change across the menstrual cycle for some people. In the luteal phase, progesterone is higher and energy intake may increase modestly in some studies, though effects vary. It is a possible contributor to cravings, not a universal rule or an explanation for every appetite change.

LAST LITTLE NOTE / NOT A CONCLUSION

One bite.
A thousand threads.

I think this is what happens when curiosity is allowed near a kitchen: I can be interested in glucose curves, fibre, labels and the next generation of food technology, and still want a cookie with crisp edges and a properly gooey middle. Maybe I don't need to biohack every meal or turn lunch into a performance review. I can enjoy the food, read the packet, wonder who engineered that perfect snap, and occasionally find myself thinking about cocoa cells in a bioreactor. The future of food is arriving; I would still like it to taste lovely.
