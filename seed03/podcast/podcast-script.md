# THE BODY, INC. — PODCAST SCRIPT / WORKING DRAFT

**Format:** solo, conversational long-form episode  
**Working title:** *The Body, Inc.: Are We Learning to Live Longer, or Just Learning to Monitor Ourselves?*  
**Target length:** 22–28 minutes at a natural pace, with pauses  
**Voice:** curious, frank, articulate, lightly funny; medically literate without pretending to be the final authority

> Recording note: This is a spoken script, not an essay to be read like a school presentation. Keep the ideas and lines you love; change anything that feels unnatural in your mouth. The cue cards are the safety net, not a test of memory.

---

## 00:00 — Cold open: the body with a dashboard

I have a slightly ridiculous relationship with health technology.

I wear a device that tells me how well I slept, how recovered I am, how much strain I have accumulated, and occasionally presents a number that makes me feel like my body has submitted a quarterly performance review.

And the thing is, I like it. I like seeing patterns. I like being able to look back and notice that something feels different across a few weeks, rather than treating every day as an isolated little mystery.

But sometimes I wonder when paying attention becomes monitoring. When a useful little nudge becomes another thing to manage. When the body stops being the place I live and becomes a dashboard I am constantly trying to improve.

Because we're in this moment where everything is measurable. Sleep, glucose, recovery, biological age, hormones, steps, stress. There is a test, a tracker, a protocol, a supplement stack, a founder promising that we can get ahead of biology if we just take the right combination of things seriously enough.

And somewhere in all of that, there are genuinely exciting scientific questions. There is also an enormous market that has learned how to sell us the feeling of control.

So this is about the body, Inc. — the body as biology, the body as a business, and the slightly strange relationship we are developing with both.

I'm also going to talk about women's health, longevity, experimental peptides, and the internet's ability to make every scientific question look like a lifestyle choice. And, because apparently I cannot have one interest at a time, I want to talk about authenticity, unfinished projects, and the pressure to turn every passing curiosity into content.

A lot to cover. Obviously, I have brought cue cards.

[PAUSE — let the premise land]

## 02:00 — How I got here: curiosity before credentials

I didn't arrive at medicine through one grand, cinematic revelation. It was much less dramatic than that. The next step appeared, and I took it. Medicine was familiar, respectable, and it came with a map that many Indian families understand very well: study, qualify, work, settle. A life arranged like a checklist.

I don't say that to be ungrateful. I say it because it took me a while to notice how much of my own curiosity lived just outside that map.

I was a YouTube kid. I could lose an evening to someone explaining a chemical reaction, a strange biological mechanism, or a question about the universe. Veritasium, Smarter Every Day, NileRed, Kurzgesagt — that feeling of having the world made a little more legible was addictive.

What I liked was the rabbit hole. One question led to another, and suddenly I was reading about molecular biology, genetic engineering, the brain, or some technology that sounded like it had been borrowed from science fiction.

Medical school gave me a grounding in the human body that I value enormously. But it also made me realise that learning the facts is only one piece of the picture. There are questions about how evidence reaches a clinic, why a useful tool is or isn't accessible, how a workflow can make a doctor's day harder than it needs to be, and how a discovery becomes something a real person can benefit from.

That's where medicine, technology, design and research start to overlap for me. I'm interested in the seam between disciplines — the bit that doesn't fit neatly in one syllabus.

And I have to be honest about another pattern: I can get intensely interested in something, follow every intriguing lead, and then leave it half-finished when the next interesting thing walks into the room.

That is not always a charming personality quirk. Sometimes it is just inconsistency in a nice outfit.

But one of those abandoned interests has become a rather excellent opening anecdote.

## 04:30 — The one-post Instagram account and the Nobel Prize

At one point, I made an Instagram account about optogenetics. One post. That was the full editorial calendar. The account quietly died, as many of my ambitious little projects have done.

Optogenetics, though, is extraordinary. In very broad terms, researchers use light-sensitive proteins to control the activity of specific cells, including neurons, which helps them investigate how neural circuits work. It is a way of asking more precise questions about the brain.

And in October 2026, optogenetics was recognised by the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel received the prize for discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.

Now, I am not claiming any credit. I did not contribute to the field. I was a student on the internet, with one Instagram post and apparently no respect for a content calendar.

But the coincidence made me laugh, because it gets at something I keep thinking about: the interest was real even though the little performance around it did not last.

The account is gone from relevance. The curiosity is still here.

And maybe that is the more interesting distinction. We live in a culture that treats documenting an interest as almost inseparable from having one. You like running? Make a running account. You read a book? Make a carousel. You learn something? Turn it into a reel. You have a thought? Better give it a hook and three cuts before the thought has even had time to finish forming.

I understand why. Making things public can create accountability, community and a record of what you're learning. I don't think the internet is inherently shallow, and I am literally making a podcast, so I would be a bit of a hypocrite to pretend I'm above it.

But I do wonder whether the pressure to package everything can make us impatient with the part where we are a beginner. The part where you don't have a thesis yet. The part where you are allowed to be interested without immediately becoming an authority, a creator, a founder, or the person who has monetised the hobby.

Sometimes a thing can matter to you before it becomes anything useful to anyone else.

## 07:00 — The Body, Inc.: longevity, but what are we buying?

My interest in longevity came through that same sort of rabbit hole. Podcasts, neuroscience, human behaviour, the question of what it would mean to preserve health and function as we age. Then you encounter the broader longevity world: wearables, recovery protocols, supplements, cold plunges, red light, hyperbaric chambers, biological-age tests, and routines that appear to require both a laboratory and a second source of income.

Some of this is interesting. Some of it is sensible in specific circumstances. Some of it is still being studied. And some of it is a fairly expensive way of dressing uncertainty up as certainty.

I think we need to separate two ideas that often get merged: longevity science and the longevity industry.

Longevity science asks difficult questions about ageing, disease, biological function and whether an intervention meaningfully changes health over time. The industry often takes those questions and turns them into a product you can buy now.

The tricky part is that a mechanism can be plausible without a treatment being proven. A biomarker can change without us knowing whether that means you will live longer or feel better. A test can generate a number that looks wonderfully precise while still leaving us uncertain about what to do with it.

There's a useful example from the current biological-age testing boom. Tests may use different things — epigenetic markers, blood proteins, other biological signals or wearable-derived metrics — and they can produce conflicting estimates. Stanford Medicine's September 2026 explainer says these consumer results need to be interpreted cautiously. They are not a definitive verdict on how quickly you are ageing.

I find that fascinating, because it shows the difference between being able to measure something and fully understanding what the measurement means.

There is a similar tension in some of the more interesting longevity research. In September 2026, an NIH-funded study reported that semaglutide extended lifespan and improved several age-related functions in older female mice. That is a genuinely interesting result — but it is an animal study, not evidence that people should take a GLP-1 drug to live longer. It gives researchers questions to investigate; it does not give the wellness market permission to skip the human evidence.

And then there is the emotional promise underneath it all. The idea that if I buy the right device, fix my sleep score, optimise enough variables, and become disciplined enough, I can keep uncertainty at bay. Ageing begins to look like a problem of personal administration. If you are getting older badly, perhaps you simply failed to manage the dashboard.

That is quite a thing to sell people.

I want to live a long life, obviously. More than that, I want a life in which I can think clearly, move, work, love people, do interesting things, and remain independent for as long as possible. That is what people often mean by *healthspan*: not just extra years, but years in which life is still available to you.

And I'm interested in research that could make that possible. But I don't want to confuse hope with evidence, or treat every new product as a breakthrough because it speaks the language of science.

The science can be exciting. The claims still have to earn their confidence.

## 10:30 — Women's health: the body isn't a niche

This is where the conversation about the body gets much more serious for me. When we talk about health optimisation, we tend to picture the people who already have the time and money to optimise. We talk about recovery, personal records, expensive tests and the latest wearable. But there are still basic things about women's bodies that we don't understand well enough, and women still describe symptoms being dismissed or normalised instead of properly investigated.

Women's health is not a niche category. It is not just pregnancy, and it is not only reproductive health. It is cardiovascular health, metabolic health, brain health, bone health, menstrual health, fertility, pregnancy, postpartum, perimenopause, menopause and older age. It is the whole life course.

Take the condition long known as PCOS. In May 2026, an international consensus renamed it polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS, because the old name implied the issue was simply ovarian cysts and obscured its wider endocrine and metabolic effects. The WHO's January 2026 fact sheet, published before that terminology change, estimated that the condition affected around 10 to 13 percent of reproductive-age women and that up to 70 percent of affected women worldwide may not know they have it. So if you still hear PCOS, that's the older term many people recognise; the updated name is part of an effort to describe the condition more accurately.

Or take endometriosis. WHO estimates that about 190 million women and girls of reproductive age are affected worldwide. Diagnosis can be delayed for years, while people are told that severe pain is just part of having a period, or that they need to tolerate it.

WHO also notes that high-quality research on menstruation remains insufficient. Think about how ordinary the menstrual cycle is as a biological process, and how much there is still to learn about the experience, the disorders, the care people need and the social conditions around it.

And here is where women's health and longevity become the same conversation. A Nature Medicine paper published in October 2026 used two very large laboratory-test datasets — together covering around 300 million tests — to reconstruct changes around the final menstrual period. The researchers reported step-like shifts in several systems, including endocrine, skeletal, liver, kidney and lipid-related measures. A separate September paper mapped menopause-associated blood-protein patterns to pathways related to brain ageing and later cognitive outcomes.

I find that fascinating, but I want to be precise about what it means. These are research findings and associations, not a new diagnostic test, and they do not show that menopause inevitably causes dementia or that a specific intervention prevents it. The first study reconstructed trajectories from large datasets rather than following every participant longitudinally for decades. Still, the direction of the question is important: reproductive ageing may tell us something about health well beyond fertility, and we should be studying that properly.

There is something deeply frustrating about that. We're capable of building increasingly sophisticated devices to estimate someone's recovery, and yet a person can struggle to get a clear explanation for persistent pelvic pain or irregular bleeding.

To be clear, I'm not saying wearable research is pointless or that one area of science should stop until every other problem is solved. These things can progress together. But the contrast should make us ask what we choose to fund, what we choose to measure, whose symptoms get taken seriously and which problems are considered worth solving.

And the gender gap is not fixed by giving everyone a menstrual-cycle graph. Data can help, but only when the underlying signals are understood, the limitations are communicated, and the information actually improves care. A wearable can prompt a useful question; it cannot diagnose PMOS or endometriosis, and it should not be asked to replace a clinical assessment.

I'm interested in this not because women are some mysterious puzzle to be solved, but because women's bodies have been treated as an afterthought often enough that closing those gaps is both a scientific opportunity and a matter of basic respect.

There is so much room to do better. And the point of better science is not to give us more graphs. It is to make people's lives better.

## 14:30 — My wrist says one thing; my body says another

I track some of my own data, and I find it useful. Sleep, energy, temperature, patterns over time — it can make me more attentive to things I might otherwise shrug off. Sometimes it gives me a prompt to rest or take a closer look at something.

But I have also noticed how quickly the score can colour the day. If a device tells you that your recovery is terrible, you can start to feel as though the day has already been decided for you. The number becomes a story, and suddenly you are living inside the story rather than noticing the actual experience.

I think that's the placebo-shaped question in tracking. The metric doesn't simply report on how you feel; sometimes it changes how you feel. And that's not a reason to throw all devices into the sea. It is a reason to stay awake to the relationship you're building with them.

For me, the useful approach is to look at trends, ask whether a signal makes sense alongside how I feel, and remember that the device estimates some things. It does not know my body better than I do, and it certainly does not diagnose me.

More data is not automatically better. Data without context is just a lot of numbers looking for a job.

The point is to live in the body, not spend the whole day managing its dashboard.

## 16:30 — Peptides and the shortcut economy

Now, the peptide conversation is where all of this gets especially interesting — and where I think it is worth being more careful than the average internet comment section.

First, peptides are not one single category of treatment with one single risk profile. Some peptide-based medicines are legitimate, important therapies. GLP-1 medicines, for instance, have changed the treatment landscape for many people with obesity and related metabolic conditions. That is not the same as saying every compound being discussed on a podcast or sold through a wellness website is supported by the same quality of evidence.

Then there are the compounds that circulate through online biohacking and longevity spaces: BPC-157, GHK-Cu injections, MOTS-c, and other substances advertised for recovery, skin, hair, metabolism or ageing. Some are linked to genuine scientific questions. That does not establish that a marketed use works in humans, or that an unregulated product is pure, correctly dosed or safe.

The US FDA specifically flags safety concerns around compounded BPC-157 and injectable GHK-Cu, including limited human safety information and questions about impurities and immune reactions. That doesn't mean every question about these molecules is unscientific. It means the gaps matter, especially when you're putting something into a human body.

Retatrutide is another useful example because the hype can move faster than its actual status. As of October 2026, it remains an investigational medicine being studied in clinical trials, not an approved public-use drug. Clinical-trial results are important, but an experimental medicine is not made routine simply because a striking number circulates online.

I think the shortcut economy thrives on a very old human desire: if there is a way to feel better, heal faster or age more slowly, I want to know about it. That desire is not foolish. It is understandable. But the market often turns *possibly* into *proven*, *interesting in a model* into *works in people*, and *someone online says it helped* into a sales pitch.

The questions are boring, which is precisely why they are useful. Was it tested in humans? For which outcome? Against what comparator? How many people? What harms were measured? Is the product regulated? Who is overseeing it? Is there long-term information, or are we guessing?

I don't find those questions anti-innovation. I find them respectful of the actual biology, and of the person who may pay the price if the promise is wrong.

The body is complicated enough without pretending that every pathway has a shortcut and every molecule is a miracle waiting at checkout.

## 19:30 — The online self: when a life becomes content

I want to come back to that one-post Instagram account, because I think it connects to something larger than my own slightly chaotic collection of interests.

There is a version of the internet where you are encouraged to make a public identity out of every private curiosity. You like science, so build a science page. You start a fitness plan, so document the transformation. You read three chapters, so you owe the world a carousel. You have a thought, so it needs a hook, a takeaway and ideally a clean little ending.

And again, sharing can be lovely. It helps people find one another, makes knowledge more accessible and can force you to clarify what you think. I'm not interested in pretending that being online is automatically fake.

But I worry about the point where the record of the thing starts replacing the thing itself. When you spend more time imagining how an interest will look online than being with the interest. When the first question is not, "What do I want to understand?" but, "How do I turn this into something that looks like I understand it?"

I've done my own version of this in a less glamorous way. I collect beginnings. I can get absorbed in a topic, open eighteen tabs, make a plan, imagine the finished thing, and then drift before I have brought it to life. The preparation can start to feel like progress. Sometimes it is, but sometimes it's a beautifully organised form of avoidance.

I don't want to romanticise that as being multi-passionate. Range is wonderful, but range without follow-through can become a museum of things you almost did.

I am trying to learn that not every interest needs to become a project, but the projects I do choose deserve a fair chance at being finished. Not everything needs an audience. Not everything needs to become a brand. And not every version of me has to be permanently available for public consumption.

There's something liberating about letting a thought remain half-formed while you work it out. You can be a beginner without announcing the beginning. You can care about a field without making yourself its representative. You can create something because you wanted to make it, not because it completes a content strategy.

And perhaps the real challenge is not to abandon the internet, or to become perfectly consistent overnight. It is to tell the difference between genuine curiosity and the performance of being curious — and to build enough patience to let the first one become something.

## 23:00 — Who gets the future?

There is one more question that links longevity, women's health, med-tech and AI: who actually gets to benefit?

We are good at inventing new things before we have worked out how everyone will access them. A technology can be extraordinary in a paper or a demonstration and still be unavailable to the person who needs it. Healthcare access is not just the price of a drug. It is diagnostics, travel, staff, follow-up, time away from work, and whether the system can help you move from a result to a treatment.

India makes that tension visible. We have enormous strengths in pharmaceutical manufacturing, including generic medicines, while access to timely, affordable and continuous care remains uneven. A digital tool can help with one piece of the journey, but it cannot magically create the service around it.

The same is true of AI. It may reduce administrative drag, support literature searches or help detect patterns. But if the tool is biased, poorly integrated, impossible to audit or disconnected from actual care, we haven't solved the problem. We've added another layer to it.

I want innovation that is useful in the unglamorous sense: it makes a clinic work better, helps someone get a diagnosis earlier, gives a clinician time back, or makes a frightening process easier to navigate. That may not be the most viral product demo. It might matter more.

The future is not fully here if it only works for the people who can afford to meet it.

## 25:00 — Closing: curiosity, without the performance

The thing I love about science is that it expands the number of things we can ask. But the thing I want to hold onto is the ability to say, "I don't know yet." Not as an excuse to avoid doing the work, but as the beginning of doing it properly.

I want to understand the body better. I want women's health research to be taken seriously across the whole life course. I want longevity science to deliver more than expensive rituals and reassuring numbers. I want technology to make medicine more thoughtful, accessible and human.

And personally, I want to stop confusing the excitement of a beginning with the satisfaction of having made something. I can be interested in many things. I can change my mind. I can let some curiosities remain curiosities. But when something really matters to me, I want to stay long enough to make something of it.

Maybe that is the tension running through all of this. We want to measure the body, improve it, extend it, understand it. We want to document our lives while we are living them. We want to turn every possibility into a plan.

But a body is not a product roadmap. A life is not a dashboard. And a person is not a finished profile.

I'm still curious. I'm still learning where to put my attention. The lab is open — but I'm trying to remember to actually live in the thing I'm studying.

Thank you for listening.

## Recording reminders

- Speak the figures slowly and cite the organisation/year aloud where it matters: “WHO's 2026 fact sheet estimates…”
- Do not imply that wearables diagnose disease or that biological-age clocks tell you how long you will live.
- Keep peptide claims carefully qualified. Don't turn examples into advice or imply investigational agents are available for self-experimentation.
- The Nobel reference is a dated callback: optogenetics received the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Keep it only if recording after the announcement.
- The PMOS name change is current as of May 2026. Explain the older term PCOS because many listeners will still recognise it.
- Trim anything that doesn't sound like you. A small stumble or laugh is better than a paragraph that sounds memorised.
