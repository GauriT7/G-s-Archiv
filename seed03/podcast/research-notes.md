# THE BODY, INC. — RESEARCH NOTES & SOURCE FILE

**Purpose:** Fact-checking companion for the podcast script and cue cards. Not a clinical guide. Sources checked for this working draft on 22 October 2026. Revisit all time-sensitive claims immediately before recording.

## Core narrative threads

1. **The body is becoming a dashboard.** Wearables and consumer tests can create useful longitudinal signals, but a number is not a diagnosis, and biological-age estimates are not a definitive measure of how long an individual will live.
2. **Women's health across the life course.** Reproductive health is essential but not the whole field. Menstrual, metabolic, cardiovascular, mental, bone, brain and menopause health are also relevant. Persistent evidence gaps matter.
3. **Longevity: healthspan vs marketing.** Separate the scientific questions from commercial certainty. Treat biomarker shifts as surrogate signals unless evidence establishes meaningful outcomes.
4. **Peptides and the shortcut economy.** Distinguish established peptide medicines from investigational compounds and unregulated/compounded products. Avoid giving usage protocols or implying unapproved compounds are safe.
5. **Authenticity and content culture.** Use the personal account from Seed 03: the single-post optogenetics Instagram account; abandoned projects; the distinction between real curiosity and performing expertise.
6. **Access and translation.** A new product is not a public-health success unless it can improve care in real circumstances and people can reach it.

## Source cards

### 1. Stanford Medicine — What biological age tests can — and can’t — tell you
**Date:** 23 September 2026  
**Use in episode:** Consumers may see inconsistent biological-age estimates because tests measure different aspects of ageing; these consumer tests are not definitive diagnostic tools and should be interpreted cautiously.  
**Source:** https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/09/biological-age-tests-tips-advice

### 2. World Health Organization — Polycystic ovary syndrome (older name; updated terminology note)
**Date:** 22 January 2026  
**Use in episode:** WHO's fact sheet (published before the global terminology change) estimates the condition affects 10–13% of reproductive-age women; up to 70% of affected women worldwide may be undiagnosed. It is a chronic metabolic condition, not merely a fertility issue.  
**Terminology update:** On 12 May 2026, an international consensus renamed polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) **polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS)**, to better represent its endocrine and metabolic features and move away from a misleading focus on ovarian cysts. Since many people still use PCOS, introduce both terms in the episode.  
**Sources:**  
- WHO fact sheet: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/polycystic-ovary-syndrome  
- Monash University / International evidence-based guideline: https://www.monash.edu/medicine/mchri/pcos/guideline  
- Endocrine Society announcement: https://www.endocrine.org/news-and-advocacy/news-room/2026/pcos-name-change  
- Lancet consensus paper: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42119588/

**Use in episode:** General context that wearable sensing is a developing research area with potential clinical applications. Do not suggest that every commercially available score has demonstrated clinical utility.

### 10. NIH — GLP-1 treatment late in life extends lifespan in an animal model
**Date:** 2 September 2026 (NIH Research Matters explainer dated 15 September)  
**Use in episode:** An NIH-funded study reported that semaglutide extended lifespan and improved selected age-related functions in older female mice. This is preclinical evidence only; do not extrapolate it into a recommendation to use GLP-1 medication for human longevity.  
**Source:** https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/glp-1-treatment-late-life-extends-lifespan-animal-model

### 11. Nature Medicine — Population-scale reconstruction of menopause dynamics reveals a physiological cliff at the final menstrual period
**Date:** 12 October 2026  
**Use in episode:** Researchers reconstructed trajectories from two large datasets totalling around 300 million laboratory tests. They reported step-like changes near the final menstrual period across endocrine, skeletal, liver, kidney and lipid-related measures. This used reconstruction from large datasets, not decades of within-person longitudinal testing; it is not proof that every change is caused by menopause or that treatment prevents downstream disease.  
**Source:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04614-0

### 12. Nature Medicine — Blood proteomics of menopause map to brain aging and dementia risk
**Date:** 22 September 2026  
**Use in episode:** Study reports menopause-associated blood-protein signatures involving inflammatory, synaptic, metabolic and brain-ageing pathways, with associations in older cohorts with cognitive outcomes and dementia risk. Treat as research associations and potential biomarker/target discovery, not as an individual diagnostic or evidence of causal prevention.  
**Source:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04648-4

## Claims discipline for recording

- Attribute statistics to the organisation and date.
- Avoid “women were excluded from all medical research” as an absolute; describe historic gaps and biases with nuance.
- Do not equate a biomarker or biological-age score with lifespan or healthspan.
- Do not call experimental or unapproved compounds “proven,” “safe,” or accessible as routine treatment.
- Avoid generalising trial results to everyone or quoting headlines without the population, endpoint and study status.
- Make clear that wearables may help notice patterns but do not diagnose PMOS/PCOS, endometriosis, or other medical conditions.
- Treat personal examples as personal experience, not evidence that an intervention works.
- Keep animal longevity studies explicitly labelled as animal studies. Distinguish menopause-related associations and reconstructed trends from individual prognosis, causal claims, or clinical recommendations.
