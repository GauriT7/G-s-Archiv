A NOTE FROM THE PRESENT VERSION

a little bit of
everything, please.

I did not arrive at medicine through a grand revelation.

It happened rather like progressing from one school grade to the next: the next step appeared, and I took it. There was very little friction. Having a medical background in the family probably made the route feel familiar before I had properly asked where it led.

I do remember my parents dressing me up as a doctor for school fancy-dress competitions, though. The oversized gloves. The apron. The stethoscope that was almost certainly too big for me. Perhaps the idea had been planted long before I knew what to do with it.

If I am honest about the decision I made back then, the word was probably safety. A respectable degree, a reliable profession, the reassuring sequence of milestones that so many Indian families know by heart. Study, qualify, work, settle. A life arranged like a checklist, each completed box promising that the next one will be easier.

It is a familiar kind of hope. I do not think anyone entirely escapes it.

But the longer I spend around medicine, the more interested I become in everything that sits just outside its borders.

I seem to have developed a rather inconvenient appetite for the in-between.

FIELD NOTE 01THE DOCTOR COSTUME

medicine,
but make it real.

Somewhere along the way, I developed the idea that science was supposed to feel a little more cinematic.

I was a YouTube kid. Veritasium, Smarter Every Day, NileRed, Kurzgesagt, Bozeman Science — people who could take a question apart, turn it around in the light, and make the world feel newly legible. While other people were watching television serials, I was watching someone explain a chemical reaction or a cosmic mystery and thinking: surely this is a much better use of an evening.

That is not to say I understood everything. Quite the opposite. I would read about something I barely understood, find three videos explaining it differently, and sometimes record my own attempt at an explanation and put it on YouTube. The confidence of a person who has grasped approximately 14% of a subject is a remarkable thing.

Medical college, naturally, was less edited.

There is the curriculum, the prescribed sequence, the textbooks that have accumulated years of knowledge and also, occasionally, years of inertia. There are teaching styles that leave little room for curiosity, and a system that can make extracurricular exploration feel like an indulgence rather than part of becoming a better doctor.

At Sion, I know I am privileged to see the volume and variety of patients that many students elsewhere may not get to experience. That does not make the monotony less real. Nor does it erase the thought that medical education can ask a great deal of students without always giving them the tools, space or support to make sense of it.

The part that troubles me most is what happens when the pressure becomes too much for someone to carry. We speak about resilience as though it were an infinite personal resource, rather than something that depends on the environment around a person. There are losses in medical training that should never become familiar, and no ambition or examination is worth pretending otherwise.

And then a patient thanks you for a small act of kindness. A little patience, a moment of attention, a willingness to explain something twice. Suddenly, the profession feels less like a syllabus and more like a promise between two people.

The medicine I admire most is not always the most spectacular. Sometimes it is simply the kind that remembers there is a person on the other side of the bed.

The dramatic moments have, in a strange way, become ordinary. It is the small exchanges that keep reminding me why this is called a healing profession at all.

FIELD NOTE 02THE CURRICULUM AND THE WORLD OUTSIDE IT

the syllabus
is not the universe.

One of the oddest things about medical school is how much of the world it can leave just beyond the edge of the page.

There are clinical questions, certainly. But there are also questions about why a diagnostic tool has not reached a clinic, why a workflow makes a doctor's day harder than it needs to be, why a promising discovery takes years to become a treatment, and why something that is technically possible can remain practically out of reach.

Those questions pull me towards research and med-tech. I like finding the pain point hidden inside an ordinary process. Electronic health records might not sound like the most glamorous corner of medicine, but if a better interface gives a clinician back time and attention, that is not a trivial improvement. The unglamorous problems are often where a great deal of life actually happens.

Hackathons, research groups, design experiments and the occasional half-formed idea have become my way of learning outside the official route. Most of what other people might call extracurriculars feels, to me, like play with a purpose. I get to try something, break it, ask a question, and discover a new set of things I do not yet know.

I do not want to become one thing at the expense of the others. I would like to understand medicine deeply enough to recognise a meaningful problem, and understand technology well enough to help build a better answer. I want to keep the clinical eye and the builder's instinct in the same room.

Perhaps this is why conventional paths sometimes make me restless. I am drawn to places where people are allowed to question the premise, not simply execute the plan.

A good question can be a form of equipment.

FIELD NOTE 03RABBIT HOLES & RESEARCH

the internet was
my first laboratory.

Before undergraduate medicine, I was already spending time in the strange neighbourhood where neuroscience, biotechnology and science fiction overlap.

Kernel Flow, brain-computer interfaces, Neuralink, the monkey playing Pong through a neural implant, CRISPR — I remember encountering these ideas years ago and following the thread as far as the internet would let me. I even made an Instagram account about optogenetics, with one post, naturally. The account did not survive my shifting interests. The interest itself did.

And then, this October, optogenetics received the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The work recognised the discovery of light-sensitive proteins and the development of techniques that let researchers use light to control the activity of specific neurons. I had once been a teenager reading about it because it sounded almost impossible. Now it sits in the history of science, still opening questions about the brain. [a little confirmation from the real world ↗]

I am not claiming foresight. I am claiming that I have always been rather easily lured by an interesting rabbit hole.

Research, too, took a while to reveal itself as something more than a distant academic activity. A drug does not simply appear because a clever person has an idea. There are years of work, experiments, clinical trials, failures, revisions and people whose names most patients will never know. By the time a treatment reaches the pharmacy shelf, it carries an enormous amount of quiet labour with it.

That is what I find beautiful about science: the way one person's question can become another person's starting point, sometimes decades later. The work accumulates. One layer makes the next layer possible.

And now the tools for exploring those questions are changing. Large language models can help untangle a paper, draft code, structure a literature review or build a rough prototype. A person with an idea and enough patience can test more of it than they might have been able to a few years ago.

That is not the same as making research easy. Evidence still needs checking; a plausible answer can be wrong; a beautiful interface cannot rescue a bad study. But the distance between curiosity and a first experiment has become shorter.

For someone who likes learning by making things, this is an unusually exciting moment to be alive.

FIELD NOTE 04THE MACHINE IN THE ROOM

a very good assistant.
a terrible substitute.

Artificial intelligence has become the sort of phrase that can make almost any meeting longer.

I still remember hearing someone in the field predict, around 2022, that the AI bubble would burst and the money would dry up. I have been waiting for that particular plot twist ever since. Instead, the tools keep changing so quickly that what felt extraordinary last year can become a button in an ordinary workflow today.

There is plenty to be excited about. AI can reduce administrative drag, help clinicians search large volumes of information, support image analysis, and make it easier for researchers to explore patterns worth investigating. Used well, it can give people more room for the parts of work that need judgement, context and imagination.

But medicine is not simply a problem of finding the most statistically likely next answer. It is a discipline of layers. A symptom sits inside a history; a history sits inside a family, a home, a set of resources, a culture, a body that may not behave like the average in a dataset. Clinical reasoning means asking what else could explain the same finding, what does not fit, and what might be missing altogether.

A model trained on existing data can inherit the shape of that data, including its blind spots. A clinician who stops questioning an output may become less attentive, not more capable. The danger is not only that a machine could be wrong; it is that a human might forget to ask why.

I want AI in medicine to be a capable colleague: useful, fast, sometimes astonishing, and always open to challenge. I do not want it making consequential decisions at the point of care without appropriate clinical oversight, accountability and a person willing to own the outcome.

The point of a tool is not to do our thinking for us. It is to make better thinking possible.

And yes, the bubble may cool eventually. Some companies will disappear, some products will prove to be theatre, and some very ordinary applications will quietly become indispensable. That, too, is how technology tends to mature: not as one grand arrival, but as a thousand small changes in what people can do.

FIELD NOTE 05LONGEVITY, WITH A SIDE OF EVERYTHING

the body,
inc.

Longevity used to sound like a question for another century. Now it has a shopping cart, a dashboard, a supplement stack and, in certain corners of Silicon Valley, a very expensive morning routine.

I first came across people like Bryan Johnson and Andrew Huberman around 2020, back when brain-computer interfaces still felt like science fiction to much of the internet. I was fascinated by the ambition: the idea that we might understand the body well enough to intervene earlier, preserve function longer and treat ageing as something to investigate rather than merely accept.

There is a serious idea beneath the spectacle. Prevention matters. Sleep, exercise, metabolic health, vaccination, screening and early treatment are not glamorous, but they can change the course of a life. Longevity, at its best, is not about collecting extra birthdays; it is about keeping more of the years useful, independent and worth inhabiting.

And then there is the spectacle itself. Wearables, continuous metrics, personalised supplements, recovery protocols, Pilates studios, run clubs, HYROX events, cryotherapy, hyperbaric chambers, red-light panels and a growing menu of services designed to make the ordinary body feel like a project that has fallen behind schedule.

Some of this can be genuinely helpful. Some of it is promising but still uncertain. Some of it is simply a very good business model built around the fact that people are frightened of ageing and willing to pay to feel less so.

The industry has learnt to sell not only health, but the sensation of being in control of health. And those are not always the same thing.

The body has become both the patient and the product.

The more interesting question, to me, is where we draw the line between caring for ourselves and turning ourselves into a permanent improvement project. At what point does tracking become surveillance? When does prevention become anxiety with better branding? Can we want to live well without treating every unoptimised hour as a personal failure?

I do not have a neat answer. I do think the distinction matters.

FIELD NOTE 06NUMBERS ON MY WRIST

my body, now
with analytics.

I wear a WHOOP. I look at the numbers. I have, on occasion, allowed a small glowing dashboard to influence how I feel about the night I have just had.

But it has also made me more attentive to my own patterns. I notice how my energy shifts, how sleep changes the following day, how my body temperature moves across my menstrual cycle, and how a number can make me curious about a feeling I might otherwise have dismissed. I have become more deliberate about sleep, movement and recovery because the feedback makes the connections easier for me to see.

That is the promise of the quantified self: not that a device knows you better than you do, but that it can give you another angle from which to look. A trend over time can be useful in a way that one isolated score is not. Context still matters. Wearables estimate; they do not diagnose. The body is not a spreadsheet, even when the spreadsheet is very pretty.

I also know people who wear the same devices and change nothing. The data is there, but it does not automatically become understanding, and understanding does not automatically become action. A metric is only useful if it helps you make a decision that suits your actual life.

There is a whole wellness economy gathering around this impulse: protein everything, supplements for every conceivable gap, recovery tools, boutique movement classes, endurance events and routines designed to make health visible. Some of it can help people find community and consistency. Some of it turns wellness into another status symbol. And sometimes we are trying to out-optimise the consequences of habits we have no intention of changing.

There is something almost comic about wanting perfect recovery while living as though the body is an inconvenience. We cannot supplement our way out of every contradiction.

For me, the useful part is simpler. I feel more aware of the body I inhabit. Not in total control of it, not able to predict every fluctuation, but a little more fluent in its signals.

That feels like a good use of technology: a conversation, not a verdict.

INSERT / LOOSE SHEETPEPTIDES, PARTIES & THE SHORTCUT ECONOMY

tiny molecules.
enormous promises.

Few things illustrate the current moment quite like the peptide boom.

Some peptide medicines are established and important. GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have changed the conversation around obesity and metabolic disease, and their effects extend beyond a number on the scale. In clinical practice, metformin and other treatments may also enter the picture for reasons specific to the patient and their diagnosis. During my reproductive endocrinology and infertility elective, I noticed how often metabolic considerations were part of the wider conversation around fertility care. It made the overlap between reproductive health and metabolic health feel much less abstract.

But the word peptide has escaped the pharmacy and entered the wellness vernacular. Suddenly there are compounds promoted for recovery, muscle, skin, cognition and longevity; online sellers, grey-market suppliers, self-injection protocols and the sort of confidence that seems to increase as the quality of evidence decreases.

The idea of a peptide party sounds almost like satire: a social event built around molecules that many attendees may know more about from a podcast than from a controlled clinical trial. Yet it is also a useful picture of a bigger shift. The consumer is no longer merely buying a treatment. They are buying into the possibility of becoming a better version of themselves, sooner.

Speed is part of the appeal. So is the promise of precision: a targeted molecule, a personalised protocol, a shortcut past the slow and inconvenient work of changing a life. But biological systems rarely offer such tidy bargains. Approved medicines have indications, known risks and monitoring requirements; unapproved products may have uncertain purity, dosage, effectiveness and long-term safety. A compound being sold in a vial does not mean its claims have survived the work of medicine.

I find the science fascinating. I find the confidence of the market more alarming. The line between a genuine therapeutic advance and an expensive experiment can become very thin when everyone involved has an incentive to believe.

Perhaps the most futuristic thing we can do is not inject the next miracle. It is learn to tell the difference between a promising signal and a convincing story. For a useful counterweight to the hype, see the BMJ overview of designer peptides ↗ and reporting on the peptide-party trend in India.

FIELD NOTE 07WHO GETS THE FUTURE?

innovation is wonderful.
access is the question.

There is one part of technological progress that I find difficult to ignore: we are remarkably good at inventing things before we have worked out how everyone will get to use them.

India makes this contradiction especially visible. We have extraordinary pharmaceutical manufacturing, a fast-moving technology sector and treatments that can cost far less here than in many other parts of the world. At the same time, a person can live within reach of a sophisticated hospital and still have little practical access to care. Cost, distance, awareness, staffing and the way services are organised can be just as decisive as whether a technology exists.

Our healthcare system is not one neat story. There are public facilities doing difficult work under pressure, private hospitals investing in advanced care, new tools reaching previously underserved settings, and also monopolies, uneven quality and financial barriers that can make care feel like a privilege rather than a guarantee.

Technology can help with parts of this: remote triage, decision support, better records, low-cost diagnostics, language access and systems that make limited resources go further. But a digital tool cannot solve a problem if a patient has no reliable connection, no nearby clinician, no money for the next step or no reason to trust the system offering it.

And the wellness end of the market makes the contrast even stranger. Some people will spend extraordinary amounts trying to preserve their health, while others cannot access the most basic preventive care. We can build increasingly sophisticated ways to extend fertility, treat disease or track the body, yet still fail to make the basics ordinary.

Women's health is one area where I feel hopeful about the direction of travel. More attention is finally being paid to conditions and life stages that have been understudied or dismissed, and to the ways hormones, metabolism and reproductive health intersect with the rest of the body. There is a great deal left to learn, but at least more people are asking the questions.

Progress is not only the arrival of a new technology. It is the distance between the people who can access it first and the people who need it most getting smaller.

The future is not fully here if it only works for the people who can afford to meet it.

FINAL NOTEAN UNFINISHED THING

spending for destruction.
spending for construction.

The thing I find most extraordinary about being alive in this century is that we seem to be spending so much of our ingenuity on destruction, and then spending even more to repair, rebuild or outsmart the damage.

We create new risks and invent new safeguards. We build tools that make life easier, then build systems to manage the complications they introduce. We search for ways to live longer while designing lives that leave us exhausted. We make extraordinary progress, then discover the next set of consequences waiting just beyond it.

It is a peculiar architecture: a structure with too many extensions, additions and corridors, each one connected to something else. I cannot tell whether it will keep expanding or whether, centuries from now, someone will strip it back to the essentials and wonder why we made it so complicated in the first place.

Perhaps the answer is not to stop building. I would be a rather unlikely person to suggest that. It is to remember that building something new is not the same as making something better, and that the cleverest solution is not always the one with the most moving parts.

As for me, I still want to know how things work. I want to learn enough to ask better questions, make things that are useful, and find the places where medicine, technology and human life might meet a little more gracefully.

I want to do too many things, which remains both the trouble and the delight.

Fifteen years from now, I hope I can look back and feel grateful. Not necessarily for having become a particular kind of person, or for having collected a particular set of titles, but for having made something that left people with a little more hope, a little more joy, a reason to smile, or a problem made just slightly easier to live with.

That would be enough. More than enough, actually.

For now, I am still curious. And the lab is very much open.
