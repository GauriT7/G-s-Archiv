A NOTE FROM THE PRESENT VERSION

a little bit of
everything, please.

I did not arrive at medicine through a grand revelation.

It happened rather like progressing from one school grade to the next: the next step appeared, and I took it. There was very little friction. Having a medical background in the family probably made the route feel familiar before I had properly asked where it led.

I do remember my parents dressing me up as a doctor for school fancy-dress competitions, though. The oversized gloves. The apron. The stethoscope that was almost certainly too big for me. Perhaps the idea had been planted long before I knew what to do with it.

If I am honest about the decision I made back then, the word was probably safety. A respectable degree, a reliable profession, the reassuring sequence of milestones that so many Indian families know by heart. Study, qualify, work, settle. A life arranged like a checklist, each completed box promising that the next one will be easier.

It is a familiar kind of hope. I do not think anyone entirely escapes it.

But the longer I spend around medicine, the more interested I become in everything that sits just outside its borders.

I seem to have developed a rather inconvenient appetite for the in-between.

FIELD NOTE 01THE DOCTOR COSTUME

medicine,
but make it real.

I think I expected science to feel a little more like the internet had promised me it would.

I was a YouTube kid. Veritasium, Smarter Every Day, NileRed, Kurzgesagt, Bozeman Science: the people who could take a question apart and make the world feel newly legible. I could lose an evening to a chemical reaction, a strange biological mechanism or a video about the scale of the universe.

Sometimes I tried making explainers of my own. This was done with the confidence of someone who had understood approximately 14% of the topic and was prepared to teach the remaining 86% to the public.

Medical college was, naturally, less edited. There is the curriculum, the prescribed sequence, the textbooks that carry decades of knowledge and occasionally decades of inertia. There are teaching styles that leave little room for curiosity, and a system that can make exploring beyond the syllabus feel indulgent rather than part of becoming a better doctor.

At Sion, I know I am lucky to see the volume and variety of patients that many students elsewhere may not. But exposure and education are not automatically the same thing. A busy ward can teach you a great deal; it can also leave you trying to assemble the lesson while moving on to the next case. Students are asked to be resilient, often without enough space or support to make that resilience sustainable.

I find the casualness with which we talk about distress in medical training difficult to accept. Resilience cannot be the only answer to an environment that is making people unwell. There are losses in this profession that should never become familiar, and no examination or ambition is worth pretending otherwise.

The patient-facing part is less cinematic than the science videos, but it has its own kind of clarity. Sometimes you explain something twice. Sometimes you stay a little longer because someone is frightened and does not know what to ask. Sometimes you cannot fix the whole problem, but you can make the next step less confusing. Those moments are small; they are not nothing.

The science drew me in. The person in front of me is the reason it has to matter.

I still want the wonder, the deep dives and the difficult questions. I just want them connected to the actual work of caring for people, not floating above it.

FIELD NOTE 02THE CURRICULUM AND THE WORLD OUTSIDE IT

the syllabus
is not the universe.

Medical school teaches you an enormous amount about the human body. It does not, by itself, teach you how every useful idea makes its way into a hospital, or why so many good ideas never do.

I keep getting caught by the questions around the medicine: why a diagnostic tool that works in a study is not available in the clinic down the road; why a doctor's day is swallowed by a badly designed workflow; why a discovery can take years to become a treatment; why something can be technically possible and still be inaccessible to the person who needs it.

That is where research and med-tech start to interest me. I like finding the friction inside an ordinary process. An electronic health record is not a glamorous invention, but a better interface could give a clinician back time, attention and a little less paperwork. Those are real resources. The unglamorous problems are often where people's lives actually happen.

Hackathons, research groups, design experiments and prototypes give me a way to follow a question beyond the page. I like the stage where an idea is still a little scrappy: you make something, show it to someone, discover what you misunderstood, and go back to the drawing board. It is play, but with consequences outside the game.

I do not want to choose between understanding a clinical problem and learning how to build around it. I want enough medical grounding to recognise what matters, and enough technical fluency to help make an answer possible. The two instincts belong in the same room.

I am not under the illusion that every problem needs an app or that innovation automatically means progress. Sometimes the answer is staffing, training, a better process or simply listening properly. But I want to be able to recognise the difference, and to work with people who can turn a useful question into something that survives contact with reality.

A good question can be a form of equipment.

FIELD NOTE 03CRISPR & CURIOSITY

the internet was
my first laboratory.

During my early internet explorations, I found myself going down a very particular internet rabbit hole: DIY biology, genetic engineering and the people trying to make biology feel less like a sealed laboratory and more like something you could investigate.

That was when I came across Josiah Zayner and The ODIN. His experiments, including work involving the gut microbiome, were equal parts thrilling and unsettling. I was fascinated by the possibility of making biology tangible, and by what happens when access to powerful tools moves faster than our ability to judge what is safe or scientifically sound.

CRISPR caught my attention too. The idea that researchers could turn a bacterial defence mechanism into a tool for editing DNA was difficult not to get excited about. Biology began to look less like a fixed set of facts and more like a field continually expanding what was possible.

I was not making discoveries. I was a student following links, watching explanations and trying to understand what I was looking at. Those early explorations left me fascinated by the meeting point of molecular biology and technology.

I was also fascinated by optogenetics and brain-computer interfaces: using light to influence specific neurons, or building systems that translate brain activity into commands. The things our minds can do, and the things we are learning to do with them, felt almost absurdly futuristic.

I even made an Instagram account about optogenetics. It had one post. The account did not survive my attention span; the interest did.

And in a rather satisfying plot twist, optogenetics won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Apparently, I was simply ahead of my abandoned Instagram account's content calendar.

This is a recurring pattern with me. I can get intensely interested in something, dig through it, follow every intriguing lead, learn enough to become genuinely invested, and then move on before doing anything substantial with it. It has cost me consistency, and I do not want to romanticise that. An impressive collection of beginnings is not the same as finishing something.

But there is another side to it. I know I can enter an unfamiliar field, ask questions, dissect ideas and learn my way around. The challenge is no longer proving that I can become interested in almost anything. It is learning to stay long enough to make something of that interest.

I am still drawn to the internet as a first stop, not a final authority. A video can introduce a question; research and a skeptical second source have to do the harder work.

FIELD NOTE 04THE MACHINE IN THE ROOM

a very good assistant.
a terrible substitute.

Artificial intelligence has become the sort of phrase that can make almost any meeting longer. In a meeting in the AI-tech world back in 2024, I heard a particularly well-placed person predict that the AI bubble might burst soon. I have been waiting for the pop ever since. It is now 2026, and the balloon seems to have acquired more funding, more GPUs and a very good PR team. Maybe the bubble will burst; maybe parts of the market will simply deflate while the useful technology sticks around. Either way, “soon” is doing some remarkably heavy lifting.

I use these tools to get unstuck: to organise a messy problem, compare possible approaches, search across unfamiliar territory, draft or debug code, summarise material I still need to read, and turn a vague idea into something I can test. The useful part is not that the machine has suddenly become a person. It is that certain kinds of work can now be done faster, leaving more room to decide what is worth doing in the first place.

AI can do surprisingly capable work, including multi-step analysis, but that is not the same as human thinking. It can follow instructions without asking whether they make sense, sound certain while being wrong, and produce a polished answer that misses the actual point. A considerable amount of the work still involves checking, correcting, supplying context and doing the unglamorous thinking that the impressive demo tends to hide. There is still plenty of gadha majdoori.

In medicine, a plausible answer is not necessarily the right clinical decision. A patient is more than a set of symptoms or an average in a dataset. AI can organise evidence and surface possibilities, but clinical judgement still depends on context, experience and taking responsibility for what happens next.

There is real potential here: less administrative drag, support with literature searches, image analysis, pattern detection and tools that make information easier to use. There are also risks around biased data, privacy, overreliance and responsibility being quietly handed to a system that cannot be held accountable in the way a clinician can.

I do not think the useful position is to dismiss AI, or to treat it as an oracle. Humans still set the goal, provide the context, test the output and decide what to do with it. The machine can suggest; the person has to judge. Especially when the stakes are someone else's health.

I want AI to make better thinking easier, not make thinking feel optional.

Some products will turn out to be theatre. Some companies will disappear. Other tools will settle into the background until we barely notice them. That is probably how the technology will become most consequential: not through one dramatic arrival, but through the everyday decisions it helps people make, and the ones we remain responsible for making ourselves.

FIELD NOTE 05LONGEVITY, WITH A SIDE OF EVERYTHING

the body,
inc.

My interest in longevity did not begin with a perfect morning routine or a spreadsheet of biomarkers. It began, like several of my interests, with an online deep dive.

Josiah Zayner's DIY biology experiments had first drawn me towards the idea that scientific tools might move beyond conventional laboratories. Podcasts then became another way to explore questions I could not stop thinking about. I love a good long-form conversation, and Andrew Huberman's discussions of neuroscience, physiology and human behaviour fed that curiosity. Bryan Johnson's longevity experiments offered a different angle. His company, Kernel, also developed Flow, a headset designed to measure brain activity.

These interests overlap in their fascination with biology, but they ask different questions. Genetic engineering asks how we might alter biological systems; neuroscience asks how those systems work; longevity research asks how we might preserve health and function over time. The connections are worth exploring, but each needs its own evidence and its own standards of proof.

The longevity conversation has since spilled into the mainstream, and the wellness market has been quick to package it. Wearables, supplement stacks, continuous metrics, recovery protocols, Pilates, run clubs, HYROX, cryotherapy, hyperbaric chambers, red-light panels: a whole catalogue of ways to treat the body as something that can be measured, maintained and upgraded.

Some of these practices have a reasonable evidence base for particular goals. Some are still being studied. Some are expensive rituals wrapped in scientific vocabulary. The problem is not that people want to feel better or stay healthy for longer. The problem is that the market can blur the line between a plausible mechanism, a measurable short-term effect and proof of longer life.

I am genuinely excited by longevity research. I also think the industry around it deserves a sharper eye. It sells health, but it also sells control, the feeling that if you buy the right device, follow the right protocol and optimise enough variables, ageing might become a personal failure you can avoid.

I want the science to move quickly. I want the claims to earn their confidence.

FIELD NOTE 06NUMBERS ON MY WRIST

my body, now
with analytics.

I have become more attentive to the patterns in my own body, partly through tracking and partly because paying attention has a way of making you notice things you used to wave away.

Sleep, energy, temperature and the shifts across my menstrual cycle are easier to think about when I can look back over time rather than rely entirely on yesterday's memory. Sometimes a change in how I feel shows up alongside a change in the data, and that can prompt me to slow down or pay closer attention. It can also make me curious about patterns I might otherwise have missed.

I know there is a placebo-shaped question here. If a score tells you that you slept badly, can it change how you feel about the day? Absolutely. Numbers can clarify; they can also colour the experience they are meant to describe. I try to look at trends rather than treating one score as a verdict, and I remind myself that a wearable estimates some things. It does not diagnose illness or know my body better than I do.

Do I think everyone needs a device like this? Absolutely not. Plenty of people understand their bodies without one, and more data is not automatically better. Does tracking help me? Yes, when it gives me a useful prompt, helps me notice a pattern or nudges me to take a change seriously. If I feel unwell, the number is a clue at most, not a substitute for judgement or medical care.

The wider wellness economy can make this all look like a competition: who tracks the most, recovers the fastest, owns the newest device or has the most optimised routine. I am not interested in turning health into another performance. The point is to live in the body, not spend the whole day managing its dashboard.

For me, the best outcome is fairly unglamorous. I am a little more fluent in my own signals, a little less likely to ignore a pattern, and still aware that bodies are variable, messy and not entirely predictable. That is enough.

INSERT / LOOSE SHEETPEPTIDES, PARTIES & THE SHORTCUT ECONOMY

tiny molecules.
enormous promises.

The peptide boom is where the future-facing language of biology meets the very old business of selling people a shortcut.

First, it helps to separate very different things that now get lumped together. Peptide-based medicines can be important treatments, and GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have changed care for many people with obesity and related metabolic conditions. Retatrutide, a multi-receptor drug being studied for metabolic disease, belongs in the world of clinical research; it should not be treated as interchangeable with a product sold online as a wellness injection. The evidence, regulatory status and safety questions differ from compound to compound.

Then there is the wider list circulating through podcasts, social media and private wellness circles: GHK-Cu for skin or hair, MOTS-c for metabolism and performance, BPC-157 for injury recovery, and an ever-expanding menu of compounds with claims around muscle, cognition, inflammation or longevity. Some have legitimate scientific questions behind them. That does not mean the marketed benefit has been demonstrated in good human trials, or that the product in a vial is what the label says it is.

The internet makes these substances sound unusually accessible. There are grey-market suppliers, products imported through opaque channels, online protocols and people discussing self-injection with the breeziness of someone comparing skincare. In Silicon Valley and other wellness circles, peptide parties have become a shorthand for a culture in which experimental compounds can be treated as both social currency and personal optimisation. It is a strange thing to make a party out of interventions whose long-term risks may be poorly understood.

The danger is not just that a compound might fail to work. With unapproved products, purity, concentration, contamination, dosing consistency, interactions and long-term safety may be uncertain. Animal or laboratory findings can justify further research; they do not establish that a treatment is safe or effective in humans. Buying from an overseas seller, including one shipping from China, does not resolve those questions. Nor does a confident influencer, a before-and-after photo or a testimonial from someone who has a financial interest in the story.

My reproductive endocrinology and infertility elective has made the overlap between metabolic and reproductive health more concrete to me. But that is exactly why the details matter: a legitimate treatment used for a defined indication under clinical supervision is not the same as an unregulated compound sold as a universal upgrade. Metformin, for example, is a separate drug, not a peptide; the fact that it appears in longevity conversations does not turn every off-label claim into established medicine.

I find the underlying science fascinating. I am much less impressed by a market that sells certainty before the evidence has caught up. The body is complicated enough without pretending every pathway has a shortcut and every molecule is a miracle waiting for a checkout page.

For a useful counterweight to the hype, see this BMJ overview of designer peptides ↗. The right questions are unglamorous but necessary: what has been tested in humans, for which outcome, against what comparator, with what harms, and under whose oversight?

FIELD NOTE 07WHO GETS THE FUTURE?

innovation is wonderful.
access is the question.

There is one part of technological progress I find difficult to ignore: we are remarkably good at inventing things before we have worked out how everyone will get to use them.

India makes this contradiction especially visible. We are a global powerhouse in generic medicines, producing and exporting affordable pharmaceuticals at a scale few countries can match. That manufacturing strength helps make essential drugs more accessible around the world. Yet low medicine prices alone do not guarantee access to healthcare at home. Consultations, investigations, travel, lost wages and follow-up can still put treatment out of reach.

Technology can make specific parts of care more efficient: telemedicine can spare some patients unnecessary travel, digital records can reduce repeated paperwork, and decision-support tools can help clinicians use evidence more consistently. But these tools only help when they connect to a functioning care pathway, with trained staff, diagnostics, medicines and follow-up. A digital consultation that ends without access to the tests or treatment a patient needs has not solved the problem.

The contrast is particularly sharp when you place the wellness market beside basic healthcare. Some people can spend extraordinary amounts trying to optimise sleep, recovery and ageing; others still struggle to access preventive care or a timely diagnosis. We can develop increasingly sophisticated ways to track the body, extend fertility or treat disease and still fail to make ordinary care ordinary.

Women's health deserves far more serious attention. Menstrual disorders, endometriosis, PCOS, infertility, hormonal changes and the relationship between reproductive and metabolic health affect millions of women, yet important questions remain under-researched and symptoms are too often normalised or dismissed. Better evidence, representative research and accessible diagnostic tools could change how these conditions are recognised and treated. That progress must reach beyond women who can afford premium consultations and interventions.

I am interested in innovation, but not innovation as a showroom. I want to know who it helps, who it leaves out, what it costs to maintain and whether it makes someone's actual day better. A technology can be brilliant on paper and still fail the person it was supposed to serve.

The future is not fully here if it only works for the people who can afford to meet it.

FINAL NOTEAN UNFINISHED THING

spending for destruction.
spending for construction.

The thing I find extraordinary about being alive in this century is how much ingenuity we spend creating things, and then how much more we spend repairing the damage, managing the consequences or trying to make the next version safer.

We build tools that make life easier and systems to deal with the complications they introduce. We look for ways to live longer while arranging our days so that we are permanently exhausted. We make progress, discover its limits, then begin again. None of this means we should stop building. It does mean that novelty and improvement are not synonyms.

I want to help build technologies that make medicine more thoughtful, accessible and human, starting with a clear understanding of the problems they are meant to solve.

I also want to do too many things. That remains both the trouble and the delight. I am interested in research, design, entrepreneurship, the machinery of the body and the systems we build around it. I do not yet know which questions will stay with me longest, and I would rather leave room for that to change than pretend I have a five-year plan for every part of myself.

Fifteen years from now, I hope I can look back and feel that I made something worthwhile. It does not have to be famous. I would be happy if it gave someone a little more hope, made them smile, helped them understand something frightening, or made a difficult part of life slightly easier to navigate.

For now, I am still curious. And the lab is very much open.
